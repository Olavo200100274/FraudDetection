Abstract: With the widespread use of credit cards in online and offline transactions, credit
card fraud has become a significant challenge in the financial sector. The rapid advancement
of payment technologies has led to increasingly sophisticated fraud techniques, necessitat-
ing more effective detection methods. While machine learning has been extensively applied
in fraud detection, the application of deep learning methods remains relatively limited.
Inspired by brain-like computing, this work employs the Continuous-Coupled Neural
Network (CCNN) for credit card fraud detection. Unlike traditional neural networks, the
CCNN enhances the representation of complex temporal and spatial patterns through
continuous neuron activation and dynamic coupling mechanisms. Using the Kaggle Credit
Card Fraud Detection (CCFD) dataset, we mitigate data imbalance via the Synthetic Minor-
ity Oversampling Technique (SMOTE) and transform sample feature vectors into matrices
for training. Experimental results show that our method achieves an accuracy of 0.9998,
precision of 0.9996, recall of 1.0000, and an F1-score of 0.9998, surpassing traditional ma-
chine learning models, which highlight CCNN’s potential to enhance the security and
efficiency of fraud detection in the financial industry.

1. Introduction
As a major threat in the global financial industry, credit card fraud not only causes
huge economic losses to consumers and businesses but also poses a serious challenge to
the security and trustworthiness of the entire financial system [1,2]. According to statistics,
the annual direct losses caused by credit card fraud amount to billions of dollars, while
the indirect losses are even more incalculable [3]. For example, in 2023, global payment
card fraud losses totaled USD 33.83 billion, reflecting a certain degree of increase from
USD 33.45 billion in 2022 as 1.1% year-over-year growth. Despite intensified anti-fraud
measures, fraud losses continue to rise, with e-commerce and Card-Not-Present (CNP)
transactions remaining the highest-risk categories [4]. Furthermore, with the popularity of
electronic payments and online transactions, credit card fraud has become more diverse
and complex, which further increases the difficulty of fraud detection [5]. Therefore, how

to efficiently and accurately identify and prevent credit card fraud has become a critical

Typical credit card fraud detection mainly uses machine learning techniques to dis-
tinguish between normal and fraudulent transactions by building classification models.
These methods use feature information in historical transaction data to train models that
can effectively detect abnormal patterns, including traditional methods such as Support
Vector Machine (SVM), Random Forest (RF), Logistic Regression (LR) [5,6]. However, credit
card transaction data usually suffer from a serious imbalance problem, i.e., fraudulent
transactions account for only a very small percentage of all transactions, which makes tra-
ditional machine learning methods easily biased towards the majority class of samples and
difficult to accurately predict fraudulent behavior in the minority class [2,7]. In addition,
the high dimensionality of transaction data and the need to protect privacy pose additional
challenges for model design and training. Therefore, how to extract effective features in
unbalanced data to improve the robustness and generalization of fraud detection remains a
focus of attention in academia and industry.
Inspired by brain-like computing, this work proposes a Continuous-Coupled Neural
Network (CCNN)-based approach for credit card fraud detection. Brain-like computing
is a computational model that mimics the neuronal and synaptic activities of the human
brain, with asynchronous and parallel processing characteristics, and shows excellent
adaptability and high efficiency in dealing with high-dimensional, nonlinear data, and
complex tasks [8]. In recent years, brain-like computational methods have made significant
progress in the fields of image recognition, signal processing, etc. Their unique advantages
in sparse data modeling, time series analysis, and other problems provide new research
ideas for credit card fraud detection. The CCNN simulates the brain’s ability to perceive
and process complex patterns continuously through successive convolutional operations
and is able to efficiently extract potential features from transaction data. Especially in the
scenario of sparse samples and hidden patterns, the CCNN shows high robustness [9].
To address the common class imbalance problem in credit card transaction data,
i.e., the number of fraudulent transactions is significantly less than that of normal transac-
tions, we adopt the synthetic minority class oversampling technique (SMOTE) to preprocess
the data. The SMOTE makes the data distribution balanced by generating new minority
class samples in the feature space, thus mitigating the negative impact of imbalanced data
on the training of the model and improving the model’s recognition of the minority class
samples [10–13].
Combining the descriptions in the previous two paragraphs, the main contributions
of this work are summarized as follows:
•
Our work introduces brain-like computing into the field of financial fraud detection,
opening up new possibilities for enhancing fraud detection systems.
•
By mimicking the neural processes of the human brain, the brain-like computing
method significantly improves the ability to detect credit card fraud, with an accuracy
of 0.9998, precision of 0.9996, recall of 1.0000, and an F1-score of 0.9998, offering a
more sophisticated approach than traditional techniques.
•
The incorporation of SMOTE resampling in the training of the CCNN model effectively
mitigates the challenges posed by class imbalance, which often lead to suboptimal
training performance when dealing with fraudulent and normal transaction samples
in the dataset.

two types: machine learning methods and deep learning techniques.

2.1. Credit Card Fraud Detection Based on Machine Learning
Machine learning (ML) is an essential tool for credit card fraud detection, offering
advanced methods to identify fraudulent transactions with high accuracy. Among these,
Random Forest (RF) is widely used as an ensemble learning technique, combining multiple
decision trees to improve classification precision. RF is particularly effective in handling
large and imbalanced datasets, which are common in real-world fraud detection scenarios.
However, its application to real-time data streams may be constrained by computational lim-
itations [14]. Support Vector Machines (SVMs) have proven effective for high-dimensional
classification tasks and are commonly employed in detecting fraudulent activity. While
SVMs yield accurate results for smaller datasets, their efficiency diminishes when applied
to large-scale data due to their computational and memory-intensive nature [15]. Similarly,
K-Nearest Neighbors (KNNs) has been utilized for its simplicity and interpretability in
classifying fraudulent transactions based on proximity. Despite achieving high accuracy
in specific datasets, KNN’s performance is hindered by its reliance on large amounts of
memory and susceptibility to noisy or imbalanced data [16]. Hybrid approaches, which
integrate multiple ML algorithms, offer a promising solution to these limitations. For
example, models combining Random Forest and Isolation Forest have demonstrated en-
hanced capabilities in detecting anomaly-based fraudulent transactions while maintaining
computational efficiency [17]. These methods collectively illustrate the potential of ML
techniques to improve fraud detection systems, though challenges related to scalability and
real-time applicability remain.
2.2. Credit Card Fraud Detection Based on Deep Learning
In recent years, deep learning models based on neural networks have become an
effective fraud detection tool.
Rushin et al. [18] investigated the application of Autoencoders (AEs) in credit card
fraud detection, and the results showed that deep learning models outperformed traditional
methods such as gradient boosted trees and logistic regression. This suggests that deep
learning methods can significantly improve the accuracy of fraud detection by learning com-
plex patterns and relationships between data. Bhattacharyya et al. [19] presented a study on
credit card fraud detection using deep data mining approaches, which demonstrated that
the method they used are able to identify fraud more efficiently than traditional methods
when dealing with large-scale data. This approach exploits the advantages of deep learning
on large-scale datasets and is able to capture deep features in the data, thus improving the
efficiency and accuracy of fraud detection. Raval et al. [20] proposed RaKShA, an explain-
able Long Short-Term Memory (LSTM) model for credit card fraud detection, integrating
Explainable AI (XAI) with LSTM to enhance transparency and interpretability. XAI helps
extract key features from the dataset, improving model performance. The X-LSTM model
achieved 99.8% accuracy over 50 epochs compared to 85% for a simple LSTM. The results
were stored on a blockchain to ensure tamper-proof, trusted records. This approach shows
strong potential for real-world financial applications. Mienye et al. [21] proposed a hybrid
deep learning framework that combines Generative Adversarial Networks (GAN) and
Recurrent Neural Networks (RNN) to enhance credit card fraud detection. The framework
generates realistic synthetic fraudulent transaction data via GAN to address data imbalance
and enhance the quality of the training set. Then, using various deep learning architectures
such as a simple RNN, LSTM, and a gated recurrent unit (GRU), the discriminator is trained
to distinguish between real and synthetic transactions, and the model is further refined
to identify whether a transaction is fraudulent or legitimate. Du et al. [22] proposed a
hybrid method of AE-XGB-SMOTE-CGAN, combining an AE, XGBoost, the SMOTE, and
a GAN. The approach addresses class imbalance by using the SMOTE for oversampling

existing deep learning methods for credit card fraud detection are still weak in the face of

2.3. Brain-like Computing
The human brain consists of billions of neurons, where dendrites act as the primary
input interface [8]. The cell body aggregates incoming electrical signals from various
dendritic branches and generates an action potential once a certain membrane threshold is
exceeded. These action potentials travel through the axons and are transmitted to other
neurons via synaptic connections [23,24].
Spiking neural networks (SNNs) have gained significant attention in recent years
as an important model in neural computing. The research interest in SNNs continues
to grow [8,25,26], with advancements such as multi-threshold augmented SNNs [27],
vform [28], deep SNNs with knowledge distillation [25], and time-to-first-spike (TTFS)
mechanisms in SNNs [29]. Recently, brain-like computing techniques have found appli-
cations in diverse areas like affective computing [30], mental health monitoring [31], and
smart home technologies [32].
At present, various brain-like computational models are available. Wang et al. [33]
proposed a Wang–Zhang (W–Z) neuron model based on neural energy theory, which is
equivalent to the Hodgkin–Huxley (H–H) model through analytical dynamics. This model
provides a new framework for neural information processing and effectively explains the
interaction of multiple brain systems. Their neural energy theory offers unique advantages
in neural modeling and information processing, advancing the integration of experimental
and theoretical neuroscience. However, one of the most poplar methods for the primary
visual cortex is the Pulse-Coupled Neural Network (PCNN). The concept of the PCNN
was introduced in the 1990s by Eckhorn and colleagues [34], who developed the model
to simulate the synchronized activity of neural impulses based on their observations of
synchronized oscillations in the cat’s visual cortex [34,35]. The research has consistently
demonstrated that the PCNN offers notable advantages over other neural network models,
especially in the realm of image classification [35].
Evolved from the PCNN, the CCNN overcomes the deterministic limitations of PCNN
models, which struggle to realistically mimic the dynamic and complex behavior of human
brain neurons [9]. With its ability to exhibit both periodic and chaotic behavior under
varying stimuli, the CCNN has demonstrated potential in diverse fields, including image
recognition and anomaly detection.
Despite its potential, the CCNN has not yet been applied to the domain of financial
fraud detection, including credit card fraud. The unique characteristics of the CCNN, such
as its ability to handle dynamic, chaotic patterns and adapt to changing inputs, align well
with the nature of fraud detection, where fraudulent behaviors often evolve over time to
bypass existing detection mechanisms.

In 2002, Chawla et al. [37] proposed the SMOTE, which balances the dataset by gen-
erating synthetic minority class samples to enhance the model’s ability to recognize the
minority classes. The SMOTE not only effectively mitigates the class imbalance problem
but also helps to reduce the overfitting and improve the model’s generalization perfor-
mance. This technique is widely used in various fields, which significantly improves the
classification effect in unbalanced data environments [38–40].
The idea of the SOMTE algorithm is to generate new, virtual minority class samples
in the feature space to balance the dataset. The specific steps include the following: first,
randomly selecting a minority class sample; second, finding the nearest neighbors of that
sample in the set of minority class samples; and finally, synthesizing a new minority class
sample by interpolating features between the selected sample and its neighbors [37].
The specific steps of the SMOTE for the Kaggle CCFD dataset are shown in the
Algorithm 1. First, minority class samples (i.e., fraudulent transactions) are extracted from
the dataset. Then, for each minority class sample, its k-nearest neighbors in the feature
space are computed. Next, new synthetic samples are generated by performing random
interpolation between the selected samples and their neighbors. Finally, these synthetic
minority class samples are combined with the original majority class samples (i.e., normal
transactions) to form an oversampled balanced dataset.

3.2. Continuous-Coupled Neural Networks
The CCNN is a new neural network model derived from the PCNN. Its design goal is to
more closely match the dynamics of real neurons under dynamic stimuli. Unlike the PCNN,
the output of the CCNN is a continuous value, which improves the limitation of the discrete
pulse output in the PCNN by introducing a probabilistic activation mechanism and thus is
able to simulate the complex behaviors of biological neurons in a more comprehensive way.
The mechanism of the CCNN is described through a set of recursive equations. The
state of each CCNN neuron is determined by five main modules: feeding input, couple
linking, modulation product, dynamic activity, and continuous outputs [9], which are
defined in Equation (1) below:

Figure 2 shows the computational process of the CCNN neuron. The CCNN neuron
updates its feeding input Fij at each time step by considering the exponentially decayed
previous input, the weighted sum of neighboring neuron outputs, and an external stimulus.
The coupling link Lij is computed similarly, capturing interactions between neurons. The
modulation step combines Fij and Lij, amplifying or suppressing the signal through a
scaling factor 1 + βLij. The output Yij is obtained by applying a sigmoid activation function
to the modulated signal, which is adjusted by the dynamic activity Eij. The dynamic activity
itself adapts over time, incorporating past output values and an exponential decay term
and allowing the CCNN neuron to adjust its activation threshold dynamically.

4.1. Data Input
In visual models, the input is typically a three-channel n × n matrix, where each
channel represents the red, green, and blue components of an image. However, most
machine learning models require the input to be a multidimensional vector. Due to this
difference, directly feeding datasets suitable for machine learning into visual models
presents certain challenges. To address this issue, we propose a method in which each
element of the vector is sequentially placed into the matrix, thus transforming each sample
in the dataset into a single-channel “image”. This allows the visual model to perform
feature extraction, pooling, and classification.
The Kaggle CCFD dataset used in this study contains 30 features, which include Times,
the PCA values from V1 to V28, and Amounts. These 30 features are sequentially placed
into a 6 × 6 matrix, filling any excess space with zeros.

5. Limitations and Future Work
Although our work proposes a brain-like computing-based method for financial fraud
detection, achieving an accuracy of 0.9998, precision of 0.9996, recall of 1.0000, and an
F1-score of 0.9998 when the time step was set to T = 2, there were still some limitations we
found, which need to be addressed in future work:
•
The SMOTE’s use of interpolation to generate data may not fully capture the complex
distribution of fraudulent transactions, resulting in the model fitting to synthetic
data and ignoring the variability of real fraudulent transactions. We plan to find a
resampling method that addresses the class imbalance problem, with performance
similar to the SMOTE, but without fitting too many samples.
•
For the optimal hyperparameters of the CCNN on the Kaggle CCFD dataset, a detailed
grid search is required, but it is time-consuming. We plan to use a grid search with a


Mathematics 2025, 13, 819
16 of 18
step size of 0.1 in our future work to find the optimal hyperparameters suitable for the
Kaggle CCFD dataset.
•
Our work has so far been limited to experiments on the Kaggle CCFD dataset. In
future research, we plan to use a variety of mainstream credit card fraud datasets for
validation. However, due to the involvement of personal privacy in transaction data
and its high confidentiality, we were unable to collect such data privately and could
only rely on publicly available datasets.
6. Conclusions
In this work, we propose a CCNN-based model for credit card fraud detection, which
is inspired by brain-like computing. To address the class imbalance issue inherent in the
dataset, we employed the SMOTE, which significantly enhances the model’s ability to
learn from minority class examples. The experimental results demonstrate the superior
performance of our CCNN model compared to traditional machine learning models and
state-of-the-art method. Specifically, our model achieved an accuracy of 0.9998, precision
of 0.9996, recall of 1.0000, and an F1-score of 0.9998 when the time step was set to T = 2,
outperforming well-established models in all key metrics such as accuracy, precision,
recall, and F1-score. These results highlight the effectiveness of CCNN in handling the
complexity and subtlety of fraud detection, where small, non-obvious patterns often
indicate fraudulent behavior.
In conclusion, our work demonstrates that brain-like computing can significantly
contribute to improving the security and efficiency of fraud detection systems in the
financial sector. We designed the simplest single-layer CCNN model for training, which
offers high efficiency and is well-suited for real-time detection. In the future, we plan to test
this model on additional real-world datasets and explore further optimizations to enhance
its generalization ability and performance in various financial fraud scenarios.
