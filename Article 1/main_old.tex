%% ─── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{abstract}
Financial fraud detection under extreme class imbalance is a well-studied problem,
yet a recurring methodological shortcoming in the literature remains largely unaddressed:
the decision threshold applied to model output scores is rarely treated as an explicit
experimental variable. Most published studies either fix the threshold at~$\tau=0.5$ or
select it on the test set, both of which are inappropriate practices that inflate reported
metrics and prevent fair model comparison.
This paper presents a systematic, leakage-free comparison of six model families ---
Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost, One-Class SVM, and
FT-Transformer --- under seven class imbalance handling strategies and four threshold
selection strategies, evaluated on two structurally distinct fraud benchmarks:
the ULB Credit Card Fraud dataset ($N\!=\!284{,}807$, $0.17\%$ fraud) and the
Bank Account Fraud (BAF) Base dataset ($N\!=\!1{,}000{,}000$, ${\approx}1.1\%$ fraud).
All threshold strategies are calibrated exclusively on inner validation folds and never
touch the held-out test set, eliminating threshold leakage.
On the ULB dataset, the fixed~$\tau=0.5$ default performs acceptably because model scores
are well-separated; gradient-boosted models lead with CatBoost at PR-AUC~$=\!0.885$.
On BAF, the fixed default is catastrophic: $F_2$ drops below~$0.04$ for every supervised
model, while the max-$F_2$ strategy recovers $F_2$ values in the $0.29$--$0.32$ range ---
an improvement of up to nine-fold.
No single imbalance handling strategy universally dominates; Class~Weights is the
safest default.
SMOTE and SMOTE+Tomek Links produce identical results under extreme imbalance
(Tomek links remove zero borderline examples), a finding with practical significance for
strategy selection.
On the larger BAF dataset, the FT-Transformer matches CatBoost at PR-AUC~$=\!0.180$,
demonstrating that tabular transformers are competitive but not superior to well-tuned
gradient boosting.
We provide a decision framework mapping seven deployment scenarios to recommended
model--strategy--threshold configurations, directly usable by fraud detection practitioners.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}
%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

Financial fraud in electronic payment systems causes annual global losses in the
hundreds of billions of dollars, and the proliferation of digital payment channels
has expanded the attack surface available to fraudsters \citep{rymantubb2018survey}.
Automated fraud detection systems, trained on historical transaction data, are now a
critical component of financial institutions' defence infrastructure.
The machine learning problem underlying fraud detection is characterised by three
structural challenges: (i)~extreme class imbalance, with fraud rates typically between
$0.1\%$ and $2\%$ \citep{hegarcia2009learning}; (ii)~non-stationarity, as fraud patterns
evolve in response to deployed detection systems; and (iii)~asymmetric error costs,
where missed fraud carries direct financial losses and excessive false positives burden
investigation teams and degrade customer experience.

Despite the volume of published work, a systematic survey of the literature reveals
recurring methodological limitations that undermine the reliability of reported conclusions.
The most consequential is \emph{data leakage}: applying resampling techniques before
the train/test split, or without isolating resampling to the training partition of each
cross-validation fold, inflates reported metrics by margins sufficient to reverse model
rankings \citep{hayat2025leakage}.
A second limitation is the widespread use of ROC-AUC as the primary evaluation metric,
which is systematically misleading under extreme imbalance --- high ROC-AUC values are
compatible with near-zero fraud recall \citep{saito2015precision}.
A third, and perhaps the least-studied limitation, is the treatment of the decision
threshold: most studies either fix it at the default value of~$0.5$ or select it by
inspecting the test set, both of which bias reported metrics in favour of models with
well-separated scores.

This paper addresses the threshold problem directly.
The decision threshold $\tau$ that converts a model's output probability into a binary
fraud/legitimate decision is not a neutral operational parameter: on datasets where
fraud scores are concentrated far below~$0.5$, a fixed~$\tau=0.5$ effectively predicts
all transactions as legitimate, destroying recall.
Yet no published study systematically compares multiple threshold strategies across
multiple model families and multiple fraud datasets under a strict leakage-free protocol.

This paper makes the following contributions:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{C\arabic*.}]
  \item \textbf{Leakage-free joint benchmark.}
    A reproducible evaluation protocol is established for six model families on two
    structurally distinct fraud datasets (ULB and BAF), eliminating data leakage at
    every stage: resampling, threshold selection, and hyperparameter tuning.
  \item \textbf{Threshold sensitivity as a primary experimental variable.}
    Four threshold selection strategies are systematically compared --- fixed~$\tau=0.5$,
    max-$F_1$, max-$F_2$, and Precision~$\geq 0.5$ --- each calibrated exclusively on
    inner validation folds, with quantified impact on both metric values and model rankings.
  \item \textbf{Full factorial analysis of imbalance strategies.}
    Seven class imbalance handling strategies are evaluated in a $7{\times}5{\times}2$
    factorial design (strategy $\times$ model $\times$ dataset), identifying which
    interventions help, harm, or leave performance unchanged.
  \item \textbf{Practitioner decision framework.}
    A decision table maps seven representative deployment scenarios to recommended
    model--strategy--threshold configurations, providing directly actionable guidance.
\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:relwork} surveys the relevant literature on fraud detection evaluation,
class imbalance, and threshold selection.
Section~\ref{sec:framework} describes the experimental framework.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the experimental results.
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} discusses the main findings and the decision framework.
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes and outlines directions for future work.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:relwork}
%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Fraud Detection Benchmarks and Evaluation under Class Imbalance}

The ULB Credit Card Fraud dataset \citep{dalpozzolo2015calibrating, dalpozzolo2017realistic}
has been the de facto benchmark in the field for over a decade, with the important caveat
that its features are PCA-anonymised and therefore not interpretable in domain terms.
The Bank Account Fraud (BAF) suite \citep{jesus2022turning}, released at NeurIPS~2022,
addresses this gap: it provides one million synthetic but feature-rich bank account
applications with semantic variable names, a ${\approx}1.1\%$ fraud rate, and five
distribution-shifted variants designed to stress-test model robustness under covariate
shift, label shift, and subpopulation bias.
The two datasets are complementary: ULB is small ($N\!=\!284\text{K}$) and privacy-constrained;
BAF is large-scale with interpretable features.

A key methodological issue with fraud detection benchmarks is the choice of evaluation
metric.
\citet{saito2015precision} demonstrate that ROC-AUC is insensitive to the class prior
and therefore misleading under extreme imbalance: a model with ROC-AUC~$>0.95$ may
still fail to detect most fraud cases.
Area under the Precision-Recall curve (PR-AUC) is a more informative measure, as it
directly quantifies the trade-off between fraud detection rate (recall) and the burden
of false positives (precision) at all possible operating points
\citep{saito2015precision, dalpozzolo2017realistic}.
The $F_\beta$ metric, which weights recall $\beta$-times more than precision, is widely
used as the operational metric under deployment constraints \citep{rymantubb2018survey}.
The $F_2$ variant ($\beta\!=\!2$) is particularly relevant for fraud detection, where
missing a genuine fraud transaction typically carries a greater cost than investigating
a false positive.

\subsection{Class Imbalance Handling Strategies}

The principal strategies for handling class imbalance in supervised learning fall into
three categories: undersampling (reducing the majority class), oversampling (augmenting
the minority class), and cost-sensitive learning (modifying the loss function).

SMOTE \citep{chawla2002smote} is the most widely cited oversampling technique: it
generates synthetic minority-class examples by interpolating between existing fraud
transactions in feature space.
\citet{batista2004balancing} extended SMOTE with Tomek Links cleaning (SMOTE+Tomek) and
SMOTEENN, which apply selective removal of majority-class borderline examples after
oversampling.
Comprehensive reviews \citep{hegarcia2009learning, fernandez2018smote} conclude that
no single strategy consistently outperforms others across datasets and model families,
underscoring the need for factorial evaluation rather than single-strategy comparisons.

A practical limitation of most comparative studies is that imbalance strategies are
evaluated in isolation from the threshold selection step: the strategy is fixed, and
the threshold is fixed at~$0.5$ or chosen informally.
This conflation prevents attribution of performance differences to their true source ---
a threshold that is optimal for the imbalanced distribution may be entirely different
from~$0.5$, independently of whether oversampling was applied.

\subsection{Threshold Selection and Evaluation Integrity}

The decision threshold is the value above which a model's output probability is classified
as fraud.
In the standard scikit-learn interface, the default threshold is~$0.5$, calibrated for
balanced binary classification.
Under extreme imbalance, fraud model scores are systematically depressed: the empirically
optimal threshold on bank account fraud data lies in the~$0.05$--$0.10$ range, far from
the~$0.5$ default.
Fixing~$\tau=0.5$ in this regime is equivalent to predicting all transactions as
legitimate, producing recall near zero and $F_2$ near zero.

\citet{luzio2024decoupling} formally decoupled the threshold selection step from
model training in the context of fraud detection, showing that this separation eliminates
an important source of evaluation bias.
\citet{hayat2025leakage} catalogued data leakage patterns in the fraud detection
literature and showed that threshold selection on the test set is among the most common
sources of inflated performance claims.
\citet{dalpozzolo2017realistic} compared threshold strategies on ULB and demonstrated
that model rankings can change under different threshold choices, yet most subsequent
work has not adopted this finding into standard evaluation practice.

In the tabular deep learning literature, \citet{shwartzziv2022tabular} showed that
gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) consistently match or outperform tabular
transformers on small-to-medium tabular datasets, a finding that motivates the model
selection in this paper.
However, this comparison was conducted without a systematic threshold sensitivity study,
leaving open the question of whether the observed performance differences are robust to
the choice of evaluation threshold.

The present paper addresses this gap directly: to the best of our knowledge, no
published study provides a joint leakage-free comparison of classical ML models and a
tabular transformer on both a small and a large-scale fraud benchmark with threshold
selection treated as a primary experimental variable.

\textbf{ULB Credit Card Fraud 2013.}
European credit card transactions over two days in September 2013.
Features V1--V28 are PCA components (privacy-preserving), plus \texttt{Time} and
\texttt{Amount}.
The dataset contains 492 fraud cases out of 284,807 transactions ($0.172\%$).
Because features are PCA-anonymised, domain interpretation is not possible on this dataset.

\textbf{Bank Account Fraud (BAF) Base.}
One million synthetic bank account applications generated via the
simulation framework of \citet{jesus2022turning} (NeurIPS 2022 Datasets Track).
The base dataset contains 30 semantic features (25 numerical, 5 categorical:
\texttt{payment\_type}, \texttt{employment\_status}, \texttt{housing\_status},
\texttt{source}, \texttt{device\_os}), and a binary label \texttt{fraud\_bool}
with approximately $1.09\%$ fraud prevalence.
Unlike ULB, feature names carry domain meaning, enabling interpretability analysis.

\subsection{Models}
\label{subsec:models}

Six model families are evaluated:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Logistic Regression (LR):} linear baseline with elastic-net regularisation
    \citep{tibshirani1996regression}; serves as a lower-bound reference.
  \item \textbf{Random Forest (RF):} bagged ensemble of decision trees
    \citep{breiman2001random}; known for cross-domain robustness via feature subsampling.
  \item \textbf{LightGBM (LGBM):} gradient-boosted trees with leaf-wise growth and
    histogram binning \citep{ke2017lightgbm}; fast, accurate on tabular data.
  \item \textbf{CatBoost (CB):} gradient boosting with ordered boosting and native
    categorical handling \citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost}; strong out-of-the-box
    performance on mixed-type tabular datasets.
  \item \textbf{One-Class SVM (OCSVM):} anomaly-detection baseline trained only on
    legitimate transactions \citep{scholkopf2001estimating}; included to quantify the
    value of supervised fraud labels.
  \item \textbf{FT-Transformer:} Feature Tokeniser + Transformer architecture for tabular
    data \citep{gorishniy2021revisiting}; represents the most competitive tabular
    deep learning architecture in the literature.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Class Imbalance Handling Strategies}
\label{subsec:strategies}

Seven strategies are evaluated for all supervised models (LR, RF, LGBM, CB, FT-Transformer):
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{None:} no resampling (baseline).
  \item \textbf{RUS:} Random Under-Sampling --- randomly removes majority-class examples.
  \item \textbf{ROS:} Random Over-Sampling --- randomly duplicates minority-class examples.
  \item \textbf{SMOTE} \citep{chawla2002smote}: synthetic minority oversampling.
  \item \textbf{SMOTE+Tomek} \citep{batista2004balancing}: SMOTE followed by Tomek
    Links cleaning.
  \item \textbf{SMOTEENN} \citep{batista2004balancing}: SMOTE followed by
    Edited Nearest Neighbour cleaning.
  \item \textbf{Class~Weights:} modifies the model's loss function to penalise
    minority-class errors proportionally to the inverse class frequency.
\end{enumerate}

OCSVM is excluded from the strategy comparison (it uses no fraud labels).
All resampling strategies are applied exclusively within the training partition of each
cross-validation fold (see Section~\ref{subsec:protocol}) to prevent resampling leakage.
The full design yields $7 \times 5 = 35$ configurations per dataset for supervised models.

\subsection{Decision Threshold Strategies}
\label{subsec:threshold}

This paper treats the decision threshold as a primary experimental variable rather than
a post-hoc operational detail.
For each trained model, four threshold strategies are evaluated:
\begin{enumerate}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Fixed~$\tau=0.5$:} the na\"{\i}ve default; appropriate only when output
    scores are well-calibrated around~$0.5$.
  \item \textbf{max-$F_1$:} $\tau^* = \arg\max_\tau F_1(\tau)$ on the validation folds
    (balanced precision-recall trade-off).
  \item \textbf{max-$F_2$:} $\tau^* = \arg\max_\tau F_2(\tau)$ on the validation folds;
    primary criterion, weighting recall twice as heavily as precision (appropriate when
    missed fraud costs more than false alerts).
  \item \textbf{Precision~$\geq 0.5$:} the lowest~$\tau$ such that
    $\text{Precision}(\tau) \geq 0.5$ on the validation folds; simulates a hard workload
    constraint (at most one false alert per genuine fraud).
\end{enumerate}

All threshold strategies select~$\tau$ exclusively on inner validation folds.
The median~$\tau$ across folds is then applied to the held-out test set.
Crucially, the threshold study reuses the same trained model and probability scores from
the baseline (strategy\,=\,None) --- \emph{no retraining is performed} --- so the entire
threshold sensitivity analysis is free of additional computational cost.

\subsection{Leakage-Free Evaluation Protocol}
\label{subsec:protocol}

A strict leakage-free protocol is enforced at three stages: resampling, preprocessing, and
threshold selection.

\textbf{Outer split.}
Each dataset is partitioned into an 80\% training partition (DEV) and a 20\% held-out
test set, stratified by class label.
The test set is never examined during any selection, fitting, or calibration step.

\textbf{Inner cross-validation.}
All model selection and threshold calibration occur within the DEV partition via stratified
5-fold cross-validation.
Within each fold: (1)~the preprocessor (imputation, standardisation, one-hot encoding)
is fitted on the fold's training subset only and applied to the validation subset;
(2)~resampling is applied only on the fold's training subset; (3)~the model is trained
on the resampled training subset; (4)~the threshold~$\tau$ is selected on the fold's
validation subset using the chosen strategy.
The median~$\tau$ across the five folds is the threshold applied to the test set.

\textbf{Final evaluation.}
The model is retrained on the full DEV partition with the best hyperparameters.
The median threshold from inner cross-validation is applied to the test set without
modification.
Bootstrap 95\% confidence intervals ($B\!=\!1\,000$ iterations) are computed on the
test set for PR-AUC and ROC-AUC.

\subsection{Hyperparameter Optimisation}
\label{subsec:hpo}

All supervised models are tuned using Optuna \citep{akiba2019optuna} with the
Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) sampler and MedianPruner (50~trials,
PR-AUC objective, stratified 5-fold CV for classical models;
single 80/20 holdout within DEV for FT-Transformer).
The best hyperparameters found under the baseline condition (strategy\,=\,None) are
reused for all resampling strategy experiments, isolating the effect of the balancing
technique from model capacity changes.
Hyperparameter search spaces and the optimal configurations per dataset are reported
in the supplementary material.

\subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{subsec:metrics}

\textbf{PR-AUC} (area under the Precision-Recall curve) is the primary summary metric.
The \textbf{$F_\beta$-score} is the primary operational metric, with $\beta\!=\!2$ for
the threshold sensitivity study (recall-weighted) and $\beta\!=\!1$ for secondary analysis.
\textbf{Recall} (true positive rate) measures the fraction of fraud cases detected.
\textbf{Alert Rate} is the fraction of all transactions flagged as fraud, quantifying
the operational workload imposed on fraud investigators.
\textbf{Bootstrap 95\% CI} bounds provide statistical precision estimates for PR-AUC
and ROC-AUC on the held-out test set.

near~$0.18$, reflecting the greater difficulty of this large-scale dataset.

distribution, the four threshold rules do not cluster on each model's curve as they
did on ULB --- they spread out to form a clear ladder. The fixed-$\tau{=}0.5$ rule
(circles) lands near zero recall in a high-precision regime, but with recall too low
to convert into useful $F_2$; max-$F_2$ (squares) sits at the rightmost operating
point of the curve, trading precision for the recall the application actually needs;
max-$F_1$ (triangles) and Precision~$\geq$~0.5 (diamonds) occupy intermediate positions.
The geometric distance between the circle and the square along each curve is the
visual analogue of the order-of-magnitude $F_2$ swing that the next section
quantifies.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

\subsection{Threshold Selection Dominates Model Selection}
\label{subsec:disc_threshold}

The threshold sensitivity results reveal a striking asymmetry between the two datasets.
On ULB~2013, model choice is the dominant factor: the spread in PR-AUC across models
($0.742$--$0.885$) is large, and the fixed~$\tau=0.5$ default already performs
reasonably ($F_2\!\approx\!0.80$--$0.83$ for tree-based models).
This is explained by score calibration: on ULB, fraud transactions receive substantially
higher probability scores than legitimate ones, so the default threshold of~$0.5$ is
close to optimal.

On BAF~Base, the picture inverts: threshold choice is the dominant factor.
Model PR-AUC values cluster within a~$0.04$-point range~($0.143$--$0.180$), while
the $F_2$ spread across threshold strategies spans an entire order of magnitude~($0.001$--$0.317$).
The mechanistic explanation is probability compression: with~${\approx}1.1\%$ fraud
prevalence and a feature space that provides only moderate discriminative signal, all
models assign fraud scores in the range~$0.04$--$0.10$.
No model's scores approach the~$0.5$ default; a fixed threshold of~$0.5$ is equivalent
to predicting all transactions as legitimate.

A second observation from the Alert Rate table (Table~\ref{tab:threshold_baf_alert})
is the workload trade-off.
The max-$F_2$ strategy, which maximises recall at the cost of precision, requires
investigators to review~$3.2$--$4.1\%$ of all transactions; the Precision~$\geq\!0.5$
strategy reduces this to~$0.06$--$0.12\%$ while accepting substantially lower recall.
The appropriate strategy is deployment-context dependent: a high-volume automated
system can tolerate a higher alert rate, while a team of human investigators with limited
capacity would prefer the precision-constrained strategy.
In either case, the deployment team must select a threshold explicitly, with awareness
of the metric trade-offs --- and must never rely on the default~$0.5$.

\subsection{No Imbalance Strategy Universally Dominates}
\label{subsec:disc_strategy}

The factorial results confirm that no single imbalance handling strategy improves
performance across all model families and both datasets.
Two concrete findings stand out.

First, \textbf{SMOTE and SMOTE+Tomek are empirically equivalent} on both datasets:
every cell in the PR-AUC factorial table is identical for the two strategies.
This occurs because Tomek Links cleaning, which removes borderline majority-class
examples near synthetic minorities, removes zero examples when the minority class is
as rare as~$0.17\%$--$1.1\%$: the synthetic SMOTE examples are so sparse relative to
the majority class that no borderline pairs exist to clean.
Practitioners who use SMOTE+Tomek incur additional preprocessing cost for no empirical
gain on data of this sparsity.

Second, \textbf{Class~Weights is, on balance, the safest default} across model families:
it never degrades GBDT or FT-Transformer performance substantially on BAF, and it
occasionally improves it (LGBM~$+0.004$, CatBoost~$+0.004$).
The principal exception is LGBM on ULB, where Class~Weights drops PR-AUC
from~$0.874$ to~$0.726$ ($-17\%$) --- but crucially, every other strategy also
degrades LGBM on ULB by a similar margin (ROS: $-17\%$; SMOTE: $-17\%$).
This pattern indicates that LGBM on ULB is sensitive to any deviation from the
baseline (None) due to dataset-specific characteristics, not a Class~Weights--specific
failure.
In the absence of prior knowledge about strategy effectiveness on the target dataset,
Class~Weights is the recommended starting point.

On ULB, Random~Forest is the only model that benefits from oversampling~(ROS~$+0.6\%$
PR-AUC), while all GBDT models perform best with no intervention.
On BAF, no strategy improves LGBM or CatBoost by more than~$0.005$ PR-AUC,
consistent with the finding that modern GBDT implementations handle moderate imbalance
natively through built-in tree-weighted loss functions.

\subsection{Architecture-Specific Imbalance Sensitivity}
\label{subsec:disc_architecture}

The comparison of FT-Transformer and CatBoost under resampling strategies reveals an
architecture-specific interaction that has direct implications for model deployment.
On BAF~Base, SMOTE degrades the FT-Transformer's PR-AUC by~$41\%$
($0.180\!\to\!0.106$), compared to only~$14\%$ for CatBoost ($0.180\!\to\!0.154$).
This asymmetry appears in both PR-AUC and $F_2$ and is fully reproduced by SMOTE+Tomek
(identical to SMOTE, as discussed above).

The most likely explanation is architectural: synthetic minority examples generated by
SMOTE introduce distributional shifts in feature space that disrupt gradient-based
optimisation more severely than tree-based learning, which is insensitive to the global
density of the training distribution and relies only on local split statistics.
A full mechanistic account --- including FT-Transformer attention diagnostics and
cross-domain robustness analysis --- is provided in a companion paper currently in
preparation.
The practical implication is immediate: practitioners applying SMOTE to a neural tabular
model should validate against a Class~Weights baseline before adopting the strategy, as
the expected gain for tree-based models does not transfer to attention-based architectures.

it ensures at least one genuine fraud per two alerts, keeping investigator workload manageable

compute footprint --- a common requirement for systems compatible with tree-path SHAP
explainers and constrained tuning budgets --- LGBM is the recommended choice. Both LGBM
and CatBoost expose tree-path SHAP through the standard \texttt{TreeExplainer} interface,
but LGBM achieves statistically equivalent PR-AUC at substantially lower computational

SMOTE-based alternatives degrade performance substantially (Section~\ref{subsec:disc_architecture}).

The framework makes a concrete practical recommendation that is directly actionable
without additional experimentation: for any fraud detection deployment on data resembling
the BAF regime (large-scale, moderate imbalance, score compression), the first step
should be to select the threshold explicitly using a recall-weighted criterion on a
held-out validation set.
The specific model matters far less than this step: on BAF, the range of PR-AUC
across all supervised models is~$0.04$ points, while the range of $F_2$ across threshold
strategies is~$0.31$ points.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
%% ════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════════

This paper presented a systematic, leakage-free comparison of six model families under
seven class imbalance handling strategies and four decision threshold strategies across
two structurally distinct financial fraud datasets.
The experimental design prevents the three most common evaluation failures in the fraud
detection literature: resampling leakage, test-set threshold selection, and reliance on
ROC-AUC as the primary metric.

The most practically significant finding is the dominant role of threshold selection.
On the large-scale BAF~Base dataset, the fixed~$\tau=0.5$ default reduces~$F_2$ to
near zero for every model, while the max-$F_2$ strategy recovers up to a nine-fold
improvement, regardless of the model or imbalance strategy used.
On the small ULB dataset, model choice matters more, with CatBoost achieving the best
PR-AUC~($0.885$) and gradient-boosted models consistently outperforming the FT-Transformer
and Logistic Regression.
On the larger BAF dataset, the FT-Transformer closes the gap entirely, matching CatBoost
at PR-AUC~$=0.180$, but at substantially higher computational cost.

No imbalance strategy universally dominates.
SMOTE and SMOTE+Tomek produce identical results under extreme imbalance (Tomek removes
nothing), and Class~Weights is the safest default when no prior validation evidence exists.
SMOTE is strongly contraindicated for attention-based architectures: it degrades the
FT-Transformer by~$41\%$ on BAF, more than three times the degradation observed for
CatBoost under the same strategy.

These findings lead to a clear practical recommendation: any fraud detection deployment
must include an explicit threshold calibration step on a held-out validation set.
Fixing~$\tau=0.5$ without justification is an evaluation failure, not a design choice.
The decision framework provided in Table~\ref{tab:decision_framework} operationalises
the experimental evidence into actionable deployment recommendations.

Two limitations bound the scope of these conclusions.
First, the hyperparameter optimisation budget (50 Optuna trials) may under-explore the
FT-Transformer's search space relative to the classical models, whose smaller spaces are
more efficiently covered under TPE sampling.
Second, the BAF dataset is synthetically generated; whilst it is calibrated to resemble
real bank account application fraud, patterns in live production systems may include
additional complexity (label noise, adversarial adaptation, temporal feedback loops)
not captured in the benchmark.

Three directions naturally extend the present work.
First, the four threshold rules studied here are static criteria selected once on a
held-out validation set; production fraud streams exhibit concept drift, and an open
question is whether periodic recalibration of $\tau$ alone is sufficient to maintain
$F_2$ over time, or whether full model retraining is required.
Second, the threshold rules examined are scalar optimisation criteria; rules that
explicitly enforce an investigation-team capacity constraint (e.g.\ a fixed alert
budget per period) would map more directly onto the operational decisions made by
fraud-detection teams.
Third, asymmetric loss functions designed natively for extreme imbalance --- focal
loss, direct $F_\beta$ optimisation --- would provide a principled alternative to both
data-level resampling and uniform class reweighting, particularly relevant for the
FT-Transformer where SMOTE-based interventions degrade performance substantially.

A mechanistic explanation of \emph{why} gradient-boosted models match or outperform
tabular transformers on fraud data --- including FT-Transformer attention diagnostics and
cross-domain robustness analysis across the five BAF variants --- is provided in a
companion paper.
