Jean V. Alves   1 ✉, Diogo Leitão1, Sérgio Jesus1, Marco O. P. Sampaio1, Javier Liébana1, 
Pedro Saleiro1, Mário A. T. Figueiredo2,3 & Pedro Bizarro1
Learning to Defer (L2D) algorithms improve human-AI collaboration by deferring decisions to human 
experts when they are likely to be more accurate than the AI model. These can be crucial in high-
stakes tasks like fraud detection, where false negatives can cost victims their life savings. The primary 
challenge in training and evaluating these systems is the high cost of acquiring expert predictions, often 
leading to the use of simplistic simulated expert behavior in benchmarks. We introduce OpenL2D, a 
framework generating synthetic experts with adjustable decision-making processes and work capacity 
constraints for more realistic L2D testing. Applied to a public fraud detection dataset, OpenL2D creates 
the financial fraud alert review dataset (FiFAR), which contains predictions from 50 fraud analysts for 
30 K instances. We show that FiFAR’s synthetic experts are similar to real experts in metrics such as 
consistency and inter-expert agreement. Our L2D benchmark reveals that performance rankings of L2D 
algorithms vary significantly based on the available experts, highlighting the need to consider diverse 
expert behavior in L2D benchmarking.
Background & Summary
Recently, a growing body of research has focused on human-AI collaboration (HAIC), with several authors 
arguing that humans and AI have complementary strengths and weaknesses1,2. Collaborative systems have 
shown that humans can correct model predictions in specific instances1, and that humans, in collaboration with 
a Machine Learning (ML) model, can achieve higher performance than either the expert or the model alone3.
The state-of-the-art approach to managing assignments in HAIC is learning to defer (L2D)4–13, in which 
algorithms choose whether to assign a task to a human expert or an ML model, leveraging their complementary 
strengths. In a multi-expert setting, the algorithm must choose between J +1 decision makers: a human from 
a team of J experts or the ML model. Training and testing multi-expert L2D algorithms requires every expert’s 
prediction for every instance5,12. To our knowledge, the only public dataset containing human predictions used 
in multi-expert L2D is the NIH Clinical Center X-ray dataset5,13,14, aimed at detecting airspace opacity; each 
instance features the predictions of 3 radiologists, sampled from a team of 22. This dataset’s main drawback is the 
rare overlap in the images reviewed by different experts. Hemmer et al.5 found only four expert pairs with more 
than 800 shared image predictions, limiting their experiments to 2-radiologist teams. This issue is also observed 
in CIFAR-10H15, where the maximum overlap between annotators is 200 instances.
Due to the unavailability of large datasets with concurrent human predictions and the cost of obtaining large 
amounts of data reliably annotated by human experts, L2D research often uses synthetic “human” decisions. A 
common approach involves using label noise to produce arbitrarily accurate predictions. Various authors4,8,11 
use CIFAR-1016, simulating experts with high accuracy on five classes but random accuracy on the remaining 
five. However, these synthetic experts’ decisions depend only on an instance’s class, meaning the methods in 
these benchmarks are not challenged to learn nuanced and varied types of expertise. In line with previous work 
on learning with noisy labels17,18, we argue that instance-dependent label noise (IDN) is more realistic, as human 
error is likely to depend on each instance’s features. Finally, we observe that current L2D research overlooks the 
limitations of human work capacity, despite these being a challenge in real-world systems1,19, where typically 
only one expert makes a decision per instance.

To promote thorough benchmarking in L2D research, we present OpenL2D, an open-source framework 
that allows users to (i) create highly customizable synthetic experts with control over feature dependence, bias 
towards a protected attribute, and average performance; (ii) generate realistic human work capacity constraints, 
limiting the number of cases deferred to humans in a given time window; (iii) generate realistic training datasets 
with limited expert predictions due to user-defined capacity constraints, as well as testing conditions subject to 
user-defined expert availability and capacity. Therefore, OpenL2D allows for the development and testing of 
L2D methods under real-world challenges.
We use OpenL2D on a publicly available fraud detection dataset20, generating the FiFAR dataset, com-
prised of a team of 50 synthetic fraud analysts with realistic decision-making properties. We provide every 
expert’s prediction for every 30 K instances deemed fraudulent by a fraud detection model, thus simulating an 
“alert-review” scenario, where experts are tasked with reviewing high-risk bank account opening applications. 
We validate our experts’ decision-making processes by analyzing their performance and key metrics such as 
inter and intra-rater agreement, comparing them to current literature on high-stakes decision-making. We also 
compare the decision process of our simulated experts with that of real fraud analysts, demonstrating their 
similarities. By simulating these experts’ work capacity constraints, we generate a set of training scenarios where 
each instance only has the prediction of one expert, simulating more realistic data availability. Finally, we test 
two capacity-constraint-aware L2D methods by training them in these scenarios and testing them under varied 
capacity constraints, demonstrating that L2D algorithms’ performance ranking is significantly affected by the 
subset of humans available in both training and testing. This highlights the importance of considering complex 
expert decision-making processes in L2D.
Although FiFAR is designed to be used out-of-the-box, OpenL2D allows users to tweak FiFAR’s experts and 
their capacity constraints to suit their benchmarks and generate complex synthetic experts in binary classifica-
tion tabular datasets, allowing for increased variety and complexity in L2D benchmarking.

Generating training scenarios.  In most decision-making tasks where a team of humans is involved, due to the 
limited human work capacity, each instance is reviewed by a single human worker1 or by a small fraction of a 
larger team25. If we were to consider the predictions of an expert team gathered over the course of their employ-
ment (i.e., keeping track of social worker’s decision1) we would not have the necessary data to train most L2D 
methods. This means that the data needed to train an L2D system would have to be purposefully collected for 
the development of said system, which would entail high costs. L2D research should then focus on developing 
algorithms assuming the existence of scarce human predictions, that is, a single expert’s prediction per instance.
In order to generate such a training scenario, a user needs to have completed the steps previously described, 
generating the synthetic expert predictions as well as a set of capacity constraints to be applied (see also Fig. 1). 
Finally, the user can distribute the cases in the training environment throughout the experts according to a given 
function (i.e., random assignment), while respecting their capacity constraints. This creates a training set for the 
development of assignment systems with limited expert predictions, a key barrier to real-world implementation 
of L2D algorithms.
Generation process of FiFAR dataset. 
We now describe how we used OpenL2D to create a team of 
50 complex and varied synthetic fraud analysts, in order to supplement a public financial fraud detection dataset 
without human predictions, thus generating the Financial Fraud Alert Review (FiFAR) dataset.
Dataset and use case.  We use the first version of the base variant of the publicly available bank-account-fraud tabular 
dataset (https://www.kaggle.com/datasets/sgpjesus/bank-account-fraud-dataset-neurips-2022/versions/1?se-
lect=Base.csv) introduced by Jesus et al.20. This dataset is sizeable (one million rows) and has other key properties 
that are relevant for our use case and common across many high-stakes decision-making applications. Each instance

represents a bank account opening application, in which the features contain information about the application and the 
applicant. Each instance has a label that denotes if the instance is a fraudulent application (1) or a legitimate application 
(0). The task of a decision maker (automated or human) when reviewing a bank account opening application is to pre-
dict whether or not the application is fraudulent. If the decision-maker deems the application fraudulent, it is rejected; 
otherwise, it is accepted. As such, the generated expert predictions will follow the same labeling convention, 1 if the 
expert deems the application fraudulent and chooses to reject it, and 0 otherwise.
This data was generated by leveraging state-of-the-art Generative Adversarial Network (GAN) models on 
an anonymized, real-world bank account opening fraud detection dataset. Consequently, this dataset poses 
challenges common in real-world high-stakes applications. Firstly, there is a high class imbalance, with a fraud 
prevalence of 1.1% over the entire dataset. Secondly, this dataset poses a cost-sensitive problem, where the cost 
of a false positive (declining a legitimate event) must be weighed against the cost of a false negative (accepting 
a fraudulent event). Note also that a positive prediction (predicted fraud) results in a declined bank account 
application. Consequently, false positives in account opening fraud can significantly hinder a person’s life, by 
denying possibility to open a bank account or access credit. ML models trained on this dataset without measures 
to preserve fairness tend to to be biased and raise more false fraud alerts (false positive errors) for older clients 
(≥50 years), thus reducing their access to a bank account. Therefore, this task entails a relevant fairness con-
cern and is appropriate for evaluating the fairness of not only ML models, but also assignment systems. Finally, 
the dataset contains changes in the data distribution over time, often referred to as concept drift26, which can 
severely impact the predictive performance of ML models.

Alert review setting.  There are several possible ways for ML models and humans to cooperate. In previous L2D 
research, it is common that any instance can be deferred to either the model or the expert team. However, in 
real-world settings, due to limitations in human work capacity and legal restrictions, it is common to use an ML 
model to screen instances, raising alerts that are then reviewed by human experts1,28. In an alert review setting, 
humans only predict on a fraction of the feature space, that is, the instances flagged by the “Alert Model”. We 
will train a L2D system to work in tandem with the Alert Model, by deferring the alerts in an intelligent manner.
To set up this scenario, we divide the eight months of the BAF dataset according to Fig. 2. The splits are made 
across different months in the BAF dataset as there are changes in data distribution across months, a core chal-
lenge for ML approaches.
The Alert Model is trained on months 1–3 and validated on month 4. This time frame was chosen as limiting 
the model’s training to months 1-2 and validation on month 3 would result in a significantly worse performance. 
However, generating the expert predictions starting only on month 5 would use only 2 months for training the 
L2D systems. As such, we chose to generate the human expert predictions, which are influenced by the Alert 
Model, in the period from month 4 to month 8. We allow for this overlap as the Alert Model primarily serves the 
purpose of lending realism to the scenario, by screening the instances on which the human experts will make 
predictions, as well as by influencing their decisions via the model score assigned to each instance. As it is not 
an active component in any L2D system that may be trained on this data, we found this approach to be the best 
compromise between realism and data availability.
By splitting the dataset in this way, we obtain a total of three months for L2D system training, with one 
month for validation and the last one for testing.

synthetic fraud analysts. We chose to create a total of 50 experts as this far surpasses the numbers previously 
considered in L2D research4–13. As such, users interested in conducting research with smaller expert teams can 
easily select a sub-sample of our already generated experts. Furthermore, a large number of experts allows users 
to test the robustness of L2D systems to changes in the expert team (i.e., teams that work on different shifts), by 
sub-sampling different, smaller sets of experts. Should 50 experts not be sufficient, generation of larger numbers

Training scenarios.  Our dataset includes 25 constrained training scenarios with only one expert prediction per 
instance. All scenarios aim to simulate a period from months 4 to 7 (see Fig. 2), where a random deferral system 
is deployed, meaning that the alerted applications are randomly deferred to a team of experts. This creates a 
realistic dataset of expert predictions resulting from collecting a single prediction per instance, as is often the 
case in real-world systems1,19. These scenarios were generated by setting the “Distribution” as homogeneous, 
the “Deferral Rate” to 1. To introduce variability across scenarios with the purpose of testing L2D systems’ 
robustness to changes in the available experts, we create 5 distinct teams of 10 experts by setting the “Number of 
Experts”” parameter to 10 and using 5 distinct values for the expert availability seed. The number 10 was chosen 
to allow for sufficient variability within teams. Combining these 5 different teams with 5 different seeds for the 
distribution of cases throughout batches, we generate a total of 25 total training scenarios. Month 8 is left for 
testing, on the subset of instances flagged by the Alert Model in that time period.
Testing scenarios.  The Testing Scenarios are defined for each of the five teams, as we assume that the team of 
experts available in training is the same team that will be queried in testing (see Section L2D Benchmarking). As 
such, the five instances of an L2D method trained in the five training scenarios pertaining to a given team will be 
tested in the testing scenarios pertaining to said team.

In a real-world scenario, the capacity of the classifier would be orders of magnitude larger than that 
of the human experts, as the cost of querying a model is much lower than that of querying a human expert. 
Furthermore, we could expect the classifier to flexibly stand in for absent humans, should an expert, for any 
reason, not be able to predict on a given batch, for example. In our testing scenarios, however, we aim to measure 
the ability of L2D algorithms to intelligently assign, by identifying the most appropriate decision maker for a 
given instance. To better assess these capabilities, we choose to define rigid capacity constraints, demanding that 
each expert be deferred, on average, an equal number of instances, in order to ensure that the assignment system 
is able to make the most of each member of the human-AI team. For all capacity constraints, the deferral rate, 
that is, the fraction of instances that are deferred to the 10 expert team, is set to 10/11, to ensure that, on average, 
each decision maker (each of the 10 humans or the classifier) predicts on 1/11 of the instances.

Technical Validation
The validation of our data comprises two parts. First, in Section Realism of Synthetic Experts and Applicability 
in L2D, we compare the decision-making properties of the generated experts with previous literature on human 
decision-making in high-stakes scenarios21,25,28,32–36. We also analyze a private dataset of real transaction fraud 
analysts to gain insights specific to the fraud detection domain.
Second, in Section L2D Benchmarking, we conduct a benchmark of multi-expert L2D algorithms which are 
compatible with individually defined expert capacity constraints, demonstrating how FiFAR can be used, and 
how the inclusion of complex expert behaviour and varied testing scenarios can significantly affect the outcome 
of L2D benchmarks.
Realism of Synthetic Experts and Applicability in L2D. 
In this section, we evaluate the properties 
of our synthetically generated experts and how they compare to the existing literature regarding real-world 
experts in high-stakes decision-making tasks. We will focus on literature regarding “Internal Consistency of 
Human Ddecision-making Processes” (intra-rater agreement)32,33, “Inter-Rater Agreement” (agreement across 
experts)33, the “Expert Performance Distribution” within a team25, and the potential for “Unfairness Against 
Social Groups”34–36. We also discuss how these properties can potentially affect L2D systems, and are therefore 
worthy of consideration when designing L2D benchmarks.
The primary goal of this section is to not only validate the realism of FiFAR’s synthetic fraud analysts, 
but also to demonstrate how the OpenL2D framework is able to realistically capture properties of human 
decision-making which are observed across several domains (e.g. medicine25,33, finance21,28, and criminal law36). 
When possible, we resort to literature specific to the fraud prevention domain21,28.
Finally, in Section Feature Dependence and Algorithmic Bias, we use real-world fraud analysts’ decisions 
extracted from a private dataset on a different type of fraud (transaction fraud), to derive decision-making 
properties specific to fraud detection analysts, comparing the properties observed in real analysts to those of 
our simulated experts. Note that this private dataset was not used in any of the previously mentioned sections as 
it contains only a single expert’s prediction per instance, making it impossible to calculate inter and intra-rater 
agreement metrics. Furthermore, the dataset contains missing ground-truth labels, thus making it impossible 
to evaluate expert performance distributions. However, this data is extremely useful to evaluate how real-world 
experts’ decisions depend on an instance’s features as well as an auxiliary model score shown to them, a common 
practice in fraud prevention21,28.
We encourage practitioners aiming to use the OpenL2D framework on datasets in other domains to follow a 
similar procedure to bolster the realism of their generated experts. Should there be no domain-specific literature 
on human decision-making, practitioners may still follow a similar process as the one detailed below to capture 
human decision-making properties observable across domains, such as imperfect inter and intra-rater agree-
ment32,33 and the existence of bias against protected attributes34–36.
Internal consistency of human decision-making processes.  When making a decision, experts follow an internal 
decision process based on the available information as well as their experience or training. However, even highly 
educated and trained individuals are still subject to flaws that are inherent to human decision-making processes, 
one of these being inconsistency. This is observed, for example, in a study involving seven experienced software 
professionals32, these were tasked with estimating the effort needed to complete sixty software development tasks 
over a period of three months. Six of the sixty tasks were estimated twice, in order to test the consistency of the 
experts. The authors find that the mean difference of the effort estimates of the same task by the same estimator 
was as much as 71%, with the correlation between the corresponding estimates being 0.7.

Evaluating the consistency of the decisions of a given expert, often referred to as “intra-rater consistency”, 
involves using a measure of the likelihood that a decision maker will produce a different decision when faced 
with the same inputs. In our framework, the probability that an expert will commit a mistake is calculated 
according to Equation (1). If an expert’s probabilities of error are consistently ≈0.5, the resulting decision pro-
cess would be entirely random. Sampling the expert decisions twice, with the same inputs, would lead to wildly 
different sets of predictions, meaning that the expert would have low intra-rater consistency.
To control the consistency of decision makers, we introduced the α parameter (see section Controlling 
Variability and Expert’s consistency), which controls how heavily the probability of error is swayed by the 
instance’s features. To measure the internal consistency of our synthetic experts over the entire dataset, we cal-
culate their probabilities of error for each instance, and sample 10 sets of decisions for each expert, where the 
ground-truth is flipped with the calculated probability of error. We then calculate the value of Cohen’s κ, a com-
mon measure of agreement between two sets of predictions, for each possible pair of decisions, taking the aver-
age value to be our measure of the intra-rater agreement. If the probabilities of error are closer to the edges of the 
interval (0,1), then we expect the resulting sets of decisions to be much more similar between them. Note how-
ever, that the relationship between the intra-rater consistency and α is also impacted by different values of β0 
and β1. Should the values of β0, β1 be 0 or 1, α = 0 would also result in a deterministic process, as such, an 
increase within in the value of α will not always result in higher intra-rater reliability, particularly for lower 
values, but we expect to see an increase in intra-rater reliability as the value of α rises. In Fig. 5 we demonstrate 
that there is indeed a correlation between the value of α and the intra-rater reliability of a given expert.
In a high-stakes scenario, experts are highly trained, and therefore expected to exhibit substancial to perfect 
intra-rater agreement (Cohen’s κ of 0.6 to 1). Note, however, that the level of intra-rater agreement is not only a 
function of the expertise of the rater, but also of the difficulty of the classification task. In a study involving a 
series of cardiological diagnostic tests33, intra-rater agreement of cardiologists was found to be consistently 
above κ =

When implementing L2D methods, it is desirable for inter-expert agreement not to be perfect. Should 
the experts perfectly agree on every instance, there would be no benefit in attempting to intelligently assign 
within that expert team. It is therefore beneficial for a L2D system that expert agreement be low, as long as the 
intra-rater consistency and the rater’s performance is high, as this would result in a high number of instances 
where we can benefit from selecting the expert that is most likely to be correct. Our dataset allows users to exten-
sively test L2D systems under high or low values of inter-rater agreement, by sampling experts in order to create 
teams with said properties.

≈1% and ≈10%, while recall varies between ≈35% and ≈99%.

Feature dependence and algorithmic bias.  This section focuses on validating whether our experts’ feature 
dependence and algorithmic bias (dependence on the Alert Model’s score) are realistic. To conduct this experi-
ment, we will use a set of real-world financial analyst decisions. This dataset is private and, therefore, can not be 
made available in this publication. These analysts’ decisions are made based on a set of features that are shown 
to them, including the score of an ML model trained to predict fraud. This dataset only contains one expert’s 
prediction per instance, and, therefore, could not be used to evaluate the properties of our synthetic analysts 
in the past sections. Furthermore, as this dataset contains only the decisions made by the experts, and not the 
ground-truth label, we cannot analyse the dependency between the features and the probability of error, or the 
distribution of experts performance. We will use this dataset to evaluate the dependency between the decisions 
of each expert and the features presented to them.
To establish a level of dependency of an expert’s decisions on a given feature, we fit a LGBMClassifier to the 
decisions of each expert, and measure each feature’s importance within the resulting model. The relative impor-
tance of each feature is calculated as the proportion of information gain, in training, that can be attributed to 
splits based on said feature. We repeat this process with our synthetic experts’ decisions, in order to compare the 
results. Each real expert model was trained by splitting the data pertaining to each expert into 60-20-20 splits. 
As the set of decisions pertaining to each of the 20 real-world experts had an average of 10 K instances, we also 
trained the models for our synthetic experts with 10 K predictions, following the same train-validation-test 
splitting strategy, ensuring that the training set was sampled from months 4–6, the validation set was sampled 
from month 7, and the test set was sampled from month 8. These models were trained by minimizing the binary 
cross-entropy. The hyperparameter selection process is detailed in Table 3.
Figure 12 displays the ROC curves in the test set for the models trained, showing that both sets of 
LGBMClassifiers were able to model the human and synthetic expert behavior. However, the values for the area 
under the curve (AUC) are much lower for the real-world experts. In Fig. 13, we observe that the maximum 
value of AUC observed for the real experts is lower than the lowest AUC observed for the synthetic experts. The 
source of this disparity in the predictability of the expert behaviours may be due to several factors. It may be 
the case that the real-world financial analysts have a lower intra-rater consistency than the generated experts, 
making their decision process inherently less predictable. However, it is also possible that the decision process 
of the real-world experts is significantly more complex than our simulated experts, making it harder to model 
with limited data. As we have no access to the ground truth labels, and each instance only has the prediction of a 
single expert, we are not able to determine the performance and intra-rater reliability of the real-world experts, 
making it impossible to identify the source of this disparity.

L2D benchmarking. 
In this section, we will demonstrate how FiFAR can be used for robust testing of L2D 
methods, under realistic conditions. We will only consider multi-expert methods that have been adapted to 
assign under human work capacity constraints. To the best of our knowledge, only one paper37 considers the 
multi-expert learning to defer setting under individually defined work capacity constraints.
We will use the same baselines as said study (Full Rejection, Only Classifier, Random Assignment, OvA), as 
well as their newly proposed method (DeCCaF):
• 
Full Rejection: all alerts are automatically rejected.
• 
Only Classifier: all final decisions on the alerts are made by the classifier h, trained according to the L2D 
algorithm to predict alongside the experts.
• 
Random Assignment: alert deferral is done randomly throughout the experts/model until each of their capac-
ity constraints are met.
• 
OvA: this method involves training one classifier per expert, which predicts the likelihood that an expert will 
make the correct prediction on said instance. Each of these are trained on the subset of training data corre-
sponding to the set of a given expert’s decisions. A separate classifier h is trained to predict the probability that 
the instance belongs to the positive class. The classifier h is trained on all of the instances in the training set.
In testing, this method defers to an expert if they are predicted as the most likely to be correct, or to classifier 
h if the probability of the instance being fraudulent/legitimate is higher than that of any expert being correct. 
Deferral is done instance-wise to the expert/model that is most likely to be correct, until their work capacity is 
exhausted, at which point it chooses to defer to the second most likely to be correct, and so forth.
• 
DeCCaF: this method trains a classifier for the entire team, by also taking as input the expert_id of the expert 
which made the prediction for a given instance in training. This classifier then outputs the probability that 
an expert will make the correct prediction on a given instance. This method also trains a separate classifier h, 
identically to the OvA method.
In testing, this method maximizes the global probability of correctness by using constraint programming.
Training classifier h, OvA and DeCCaF.  Of the aforementioned baselines, only OvA and DeCCaF can strictly 
be considered L2D methods, as they estimate human performance to optimize deferral. Both OvA and DeCCaF 
use a separate ML classifier as the automated decision-maker in their algorithm. Consequently, to reduce the 
differences between the baselines, the “Only Classifier”, “OvA” and “DeCCaF” approaches will share the same 
classifier h. The “Full Rejection” and “Random Assignment” approaches do not require the training of any addi-
tional models.

Deferral Results.  Having determined that the models are able to model both the probability of fraud and the 
human probability of error, we conduct an evaluation of the quality of deferral of each system. The quality of 
deferral will be measured by the average misclassification cost incurred over the entire test set. The results are 
displayed in Table 7 with a 95% confidence interval.
As expected, the worst option is Full Rejection, as all possible decision makers (experts and classifier h), have 
a lower misclassification cost over the test set. This is evidenced by the “Only Classifier h” column, as well as pan-
el’a’ of Fig. 10. Experts have, on average, a higher performance than the Classifier h, and, therefore the “Random 
Assignment” baseline is the best non-L2D baseline.
Finally we observe that both OvA and DeCCaF obtain significantly better results than all non-L2D baselines, 
with the methods achieving an average 11% and 15% decrease, respectively, in the misclassification cost over 
the Random Assignment baseline. Note that while DeCCaF significantly outperforms the OvA approach for 
“team_1” and “team_5”, there is overlap between the error bars within the rest of the scenarios.
These results demonstrate the importance of testing L2D systems with complex expert behavior under a 
wide variety of scenarios, as even the relative ranking of these algorithms can be impacted by changes in the 
composition of the team.
Limitations of OpenL2D and FiFAR. 
A key restriction of our framework is that the creation of realistic 
synthetic experts requires an understanding of human decision-making literature. Over the course of this paper, 
we aimed to explain the reasoning behind each parameter involved in the data generation process, as well as its 
relationship to certain properties of human decision-making teams such as intra and inter-rater agreement and 
biases against protected groups. Should an OpenL2D user find there to be a lack of literature regarding their spe-
cific use case or domain, they can still apply the framework to their dataset, as long as human decision-making 
traits observed across a wide variety of domains are taken into account (i.e, human experts often display biases, 
inter-rater, and intra-rater agreement should not be perfect, human decisions tend to be more dependent on fea-
tures that correlate highly with the label, etc.)
Another critical limitation of our proposed data generation framework is its exclusive focus on binary classi-
fication problems. The choice to focus its design on these scenarios comes down to a few factors. First, the binary 
classification setting allows us to establish an intuitive relationship between the probabilities of false positive and 
false negative mistakes, as stated in the Section Synthetic Expert Generation: “The weights are shared in both 
formulas, with opposite signs, ensuring that if a feature contributes to increase the FP probability, it will, in turn, 
decrease the FN probability. We choose to enforce this restriction in order to produce a more intuitive 
decision-making process: if an expert does more FPs in a given region of the feature space, we also expect them 
to do fewer FNs, assuming they will be biased towards classifying more instances as positive in said region.”. 
However, the same logic cannot be directly applied to the multiclass setting, as failing to correctly classify an 
instance does not necessarily imply the selection of a specific class. While one could easily apply our label noise 
approach to a multiclass problem, by, for example, generating a single probability of error for each instance, and 
sampling a random incorrect class with said probability, this would not be realistic. In the commonly used 
CIFAR-1016 dataset, it is fair to assume that a human annotator, upon observing an image belonging to the 
“truck” category, is more likely to misclassify said image as an “automobile” rather than a “dog”. As such, realistic 
simulation of human error on a multi-class classification task is significantly more complex than the binary 
scenario, as, for each possible incorrect class e, this would involve the generation of an adequate

Our framework is also limited to tabular data. While application of this framework to image or text data-
sets could, in practice, be achieved by converting said data to vector embeddings, we discourage this approach 
altogether, as responsible and realistic use of our framework requires an understanding of the meaning of each 
feature, while vector embeddings are, by their very nature, uninterpretable.
Due to the use of a feature-dependent label-noise approach17,18, our framework is not applicable to 
decision-making tasks where no ground truth labels are present. However, we consider the generation of syn-
thetic experts in these scenarios outside of the scope of this work, as current research on L2D hinges on the 
existence of ground-truth labels, as well as human decisions. While there is work considering the existence of 
scarce human labels, tackling such issues by using either meta-learning or active learning13,40, the assumption 
that ground-truth labels exist is common to all cited works, and is considered necessary for an L2D algorithm to 
be able to learn to estimate the human’s probability of correctness for a given instance.
We also acknowledge that the process of tweaking the parameters of our framework until the desired behav-
ior is achieved is time-consuming, as the interaction between each variable is not trivial. While a real-time, inter-
active visualization of the impact of each change on the expert team (i.e., by controlling each parameter with 
sliders and viewing a change’s impact on key metrics such as inter and intra rater agreement) would be ideal, this 
is impossible due to computational restrictions. The process of generating a team of experts, ensuring the desired 
performance via the methods described in Controlling Expert Performance, as well as calculating statistics such 
as performance and inter-rater agreement in a significant portion of the dataset is far too time-consuming to 
allow for a real-time display of a simulation’s results. However, to address this issue in the best manner possible, 
we have created a notebook which can be used for analysis of the team’s properties, displaying statistics on inter 
and intra-rater agreement, performance, bias against each protected attribute and dependence on each feature 
(see Section Code availability). This notebook is completely dataset and generation agnostic, meaning that inde-
pendently of the dataset used and the generation parameters, users can run this notebook after each change in 
the framework’s parameters to observe key statistics regarding their simulated experts’ decision-making proper-
ties, displaying statistics on inter and intra-rater agreement, performance, bias against each protected attribute 
and dependence on each feature.
Regarding FiFAR as a ready-to-use dataset, uncritical use of FiFAR (i.e. without conducting proper robust-
ness analysis in changes to deferral rate, expert availability and workload distribution) could yield unreliable 
reporting of an L2D system’s performance. Using our experimental setting as an example, (see section L2D 
Benchmarking), should a user only conduct testing with the experts present in “team_1” (see Deferral Results), 
they could erroneously cconclude that DeCCaF is a clear improvement over the OvA method. In our experi-
ments, however, we show that the relative performance of these two algorithms is significantly impacted by the 
choice of experts, implying that such definitive claims can not be made about the relative performance of these 
algorithms. We encourage practitioners using our framework to conduct similarly rigorous testing by fully using 
the resources made available in FiFAR, including the extensive training and testing scenarios.
Finally, we wish to emphasize that our synthetic experts can not replace, in any way, humans involved in 
HAIC systems, as it would be necessary to gather real-world expert data in order to train the system to be used 
in a real-world application. OpenL2D and FiFAR are meant to be used as a benchmarking tool only, allowing for 
the study of new L2D approaches on better synthetic data than currently used. It could be argued, however, that 
by using these synthetic experts for research purposes, we are affecting the livelihood of large scale annotation 
workers (i.e. MKTurk), which researchers often resort to. However, in some use cases, where domain-specific 
expertise is needed, such annotation services may not be of use to practitioners, and it may be impossible/unfea-
sible to obtain real human expert data. In cases where human predictions are accessible and pertinent to the use 
case, researchers should prefer these over synthetic expert predictions, as these would constitute real human 
behavior. For these reasons we believe that our framework motivates the use of more complex synthetic expert 
data only when real human predictions are unattainable, without posing a threat to current existing annotation 
services.
Usage Notes
The FiFAR dataset is publicly accessible at https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.28351172. In order to ensure 
reproducibility, the Alert Model, as well as all models used in the L2D benchmark are also made available. 
The FiFAR dataset can be reproducibly re-generated by running the code detailed in Section Code availability. 
Users are encouraged to modify the dataset and adapt it to their use case, making full use of the flexibility of our 
framework. Evaluation notebooks are included in order to validate the quality of the generated synthetic experts. 
These contain the code used to conduct the experiments presented in the Technical Validation Section, which 
will be detailed in the Code availability Section.
Code availability
The OpenL2D synthetic data generation framework will be made available for public use in GitHub (https://
github.com/feedzai/openl2d/tree/main). In this repository, we also include the code needed to train the Alert 
Model used, and to perform all the data processing needed for the generation of the FiFAR dataset. The repository 
also contains the model object for the Alert Model, allowing for reproducible generation of the synthetic expert 
predictions.
Furthermore, we also include the results of the benchmark conducted in Section L2D Benchmarking in the 
repository, allowing users to thoroughly scrutinize the deferral methods included and to ensure reproducibility. 
In this way, our work can function as a constantly expanding benchmark by simply adding new methods and their 
results to the existing resources.
