%% ─── Abstract ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\begin{abstract}
Under which conditions do simple tree-based models suffice for financial fraud
detection, and how do we know? Gradient-boosted decision trees routinely match
the in-domain performance of tabular transformers such as the FT-Transformer
on fraud benchmarks, but the literature has not established whether this parity
is robust under common imbalance interventions, under distribution shift, or
whether a more sophisticated architecture could in principle exceed the
observed performance ceiling.
This paper provides a mechanistic account through four complementary analyses
on the Bank Account Fraud (BAF) suite of six distributional variants.
First, an imbalance-sensitivity analysis shows that SMOTE reduces FT-Transformer
PR-AUC by $41\%$ on BAF while CatBoost loses only $14\%$ ---
the attention mechanism is exposed to a distorted joint feature distribution
that the axis-aligned splits of gradient-boosted trees tolerate.
Second, a zero-shot cross-domain transfer experiment (BAF Base to Variants~I--V)
shows that Random Forest \emph{improves} its PR-AUC on four of five variants
despite a lower in-domain score, while all other models --- including FT-Transformer
--- degrade; the result confirms a theoretically predicted advantage of bagging's
feature subsampling as a structural regularizer.
Third, Jaccard analysis of SHAP top-$10$ feature rankings shows that LGBM's
attribution is perfectly stable across all six BAF datasets
(Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$), so observed degradation reflects changes in the
feature--label relationship, not in which features are informative.
Fourth, an attention diagnostic on $200\,000$ BAF samples reveals a Foggy Vision
pattern (normalised entropy $H_{\text{norm}}\,=\,0.976$) and a convergent-validity
result: independent SHAP attribution and attention weights identify the same
top features, providing cross-method evidence that the $0.18$ PR-AUC ceiling
is a signal-space ceiling and not an architectural failure.
Together with the $\sim 20\times$ training and $\sim 37\times$ tuning-time
advantages of LGBM over the FT-Transformer documented in a companion paper,
the five converging lines of evidence show that simple tree-based models are
sufficient for BAF-scale fraud detection: there is no resampling penalty to
absorb, no cross-domain advantage to forgo, no explanation infrastructure
to retrain under shift, and no headroom above the data-imposed ceiling that
a more powerful architecture could exploit.
\end{abstract}

\begin{highlights}

\end{highlights}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Financial fraud is one of the most consequential applications of machine
learning under extreme class imbalance~\citep{rymantubb2018survey,hilal2022financial},
and the choice of model family is a question that practitioners face directly:
should they invest in attention-based tabular architectures such as the
FT-Transformer~\citep{gorishniy2021revisiting}, or do simple
gradient-boosted decision trees (GBDTs) suffice?
A companion study~\citep{caixeiro2025threshold} established that, on two
structurally distinct fraud benchmarks, CatBoost and LGBM match the
FT-Transformer's in-domain PR-AUC and that the decision threshold is a more
consequential design variable than the choice of model.
That result settles the in-domain accuracy question but leaves three deeper ones
unanswered: would the parity survive common interventions (resampling)?
Would it hold under distribution shift (cross-domain transfer)?
And, even if it did, could a more sophisticated architecture in principle
exceed the observed performance ceiling on a richer feature representation
or with more data --- or is the ceiling intrinsic to the data?

These three questions are the practical fulcrum on which the "transformer
vs.\ tree" debate turns: parity that breaks under realistic deployment
conditions, or a parity that is structurally fragile, would tip the balance in
favour of investing in transformers despite the higher compute cost.
The existing literature on tabular deep learning vs.\ GBDTs --- including
the FT-Transformer's own evaluation~\citep{gorishniy2021revisiting}, the
broader benchmarks of~\citet{shwartzziv2022tabular} and~\citet{borisov2022deep},
and~\citet{grinsztajn2022tree}'s structural analysis --- focuses almost entirely
on in-distribution accuracy comparisons.
The \emph{mechanisms} behind the empirical regularity, and its persistence
under shift, have not been systematically tested on fraud data.
On the interpretability side, SHAP~\citep{lundberg2017unified} has been applied
to individual fraud models, but \emph{convergent validity} between SHAP and
an architecturally different attribution method --- such as attention
weights --- has not been reported on a fraud benchmark.
That gap is particularly consequential because, without independent
cross-method validation, a single attribution method can be confounded by
architectural biases or by the explainer's own independence assumptions.

This paper resolves all three open questions through four mechanistic
analyses on the Bank Account Fraud (BAF) suite of six distributional
variants, supporting an integrated answer to the title question:
\begin{enumerate}[label=C\arabic*.]
  \item \textbf{No resampling penalty.} A direct comparison of FT-Transformer
    and CatBoost across seven class imbalance strategies on two datasets
    shows that SMOTE collapses FT-Transformer PR-AUC on BAF by $41\%$ while
    CatBoost loses only $14\%$. Tree-based models survive an intervention that
    the attention mechanism does not.
  \item \textbf{Cross-domain parity or advantage.} Zero-shot transfer from
    BAF Base to five distributional variants shows that Random Forest
    \emph{improves} on four of five variants despite a lower in-domain score,
    while all other supervised models --- including FT-Transformer --- degrade.
    Tree-based ensembles match or beat the transformer under shift.
  \item \textbf{Stable, deployable explanations.} Jaccard analysis of SHAP top-$10$
    feature attributions across all six BAF datasets shows that LGBM's ranking
    is identical (Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$). Tree-based explanations do not need
    to be regenerated when the data distribution shifts.
  \item \textbf{No headroom above the data-imposed ceiling.} Attention
    diagnostics on $200\,000$ BAF samples reveal a Foggy Vision pattern
    (normalised entropy $H_{\text{norm}}\,=\,0.976$, ruling out all four
    candidate pathological patterns) and a convergent-validity result:
    independent SHAP attribution and attention weights identify the same top
    features. The $0.18$ PR-AUC ceiling is a property of the feature space,
    not of any model.
\end{enumerate}
Combined with the well-documented $\sim 20\times$ training and $\sim 37\times$
tuning-time advantages of LGBM over the FT-Transformer~\citep{caixeiro2025threshold},
the four mechanistic findings converge on a single conclusion: for BAF-scale
financial fraud detection, simple tree-based models are sufficient.

The rest of the paper is structured as follows.
Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews tabular deep learning, distribution shift
in fraud, and the methodology of convergent validity.
Section~\ref{sec:setup} describes the experimental setup.
Section~\ref{sec:results} presents the four analyses,
Section~\ref{sec:discussion} interprets the results and integrates them in
\S\ref{sec:disc_suffice} into the "simple trees suffice" argument, and
Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

%% ─── 2. Related Work ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tabular Deep Learning vs.\ Gradient-Boosted Trees}
\label{sec:rw_tabular}

The ascent of GBDTs as the dominant method on tabular benchmarks --- driven by
LightGBM~\citep{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost~\citep{prokhorenkova2018catboost}
--- was challenged by a wave of deep learning models designed specifically for
tabular data, including TabTransformer~\citep{huang2020tabtransformer},
SAINT~\citep{somepalli2021saint}, and FT-Transformer~\citep{gorishniy2021revisiting}.
Empirical evaluations, however, have repeatedly found that the deep
learning advantage is limited or benchmark-specific.
\citet{shwartzziv2022tabular} benchmark several recent tabular DL architectures
against XGBoost on eleven datasets and find that the advantage of deep
learning is inconsistent, appearing mainly on large numerical-feature datasets
and disappearing once cross-dataset variance is taken into account.
\citet{borisov2022deep} reach a similar conclusion in a broader survey,
emphasising that the advantage of deep learning over tree ensembles on
tabular data is not yet established, particularly under distribution shift or
limited labelled data --- two conditions that characterise production fraud
detection.

\citet{grinsztajn2022tree} provide the most direct structural analysis of why
tree-based models persistently outperform deep learning on tabular data.
They identify three structural properties of tabular data that systematically
favour trees: (i) the prevalence of irregular, axis-aligned decision boundaries
that match the splitting structure of trees but are inefficient for
gradient-based learners with smooth activation functions; (ii) the high
proportion of uninformative or weakly informative features, against which trees
are robust by virtue of their feature-selection behaviour at each split;
and (iii) scale invariance --- trees are unaffected by monotone feature
transformations, whereas neural networks require careful preprocessing.
Three properties of fraud data make these factors especially acute.
First, fraud signal is concentrated in a small number of high-leverage features
(in BAF: \texttt{device\_os}, \texttt{housing\_status}, \texttt{phone\_home\_valid})
within a larger pool of weak signals, exactly the regime in which trees
shine~\citep{grinsztajn2022tree,jesus2022turning}.
Second, BAF is categorical-heavy ($5$ categorical out of $30$ features,
including binary and low-cardinality variables that one-hot expand to many
sparse columns), which is precisely the configuration that the FT-Transformer's
own evaluation~\citep{gorishniy2021revisiting} flags as least favourable for
tabular transformers.
Third, the fraud-vs-legitimate decision is intrinsically non-monotone in many
features (e.g.\ both very young and very long-tenured account histories may
indicate higher risk), again matching tree-based modelling assumptions better
than the smooth representations learned by attention-based architectures.

Despite this substantial body of evidence in favour of trees, the
\emph{mechanisms} that drive GBDT advantages on fraud data --- especially under
class imbalance interventions and distribution shift --- have received little
direct attention.
The existing literature compares accuracy in-domain, but does not separate
the question of \emph{whether} GBDTs match transformers from the question of
\emph{why} they match and \emph{under what conditions} the parity holds.
This paper fills that gap with a four-pronged mechanistic analysis: it shows
that the FT-Transformer is systematically more sensitive to SMOTE than CatBoost
(an architectural-level fragility), that Random Forest --- a simpler
bagging-based ensemble --- outperforms gradient-boosted models and the
transformer in cross-domain transfer (an ensemble-level robustness), that
LGBM's SHAP attribution is perfectly stable across distributional shifts
(a deployment-level stability), and that the FT-Transformer's attention
distribution agrees with the SHAP ranking on the same data (a methodological
convergent-validity result that bounds the architectural-improvement headroom
above the observed ceiling).

\subsection{Distribution Shift and Cross-Domain Robustness in Fraud Detection}
\label{sec:rw_shift}

Production fraud detection systems face continuous distribution shift:
fraud patterns evolve as fraudsters adapt to defences, and models trained
at one institution are frequently deployed in new environments or on more
recent transaction streams~\citep{dalpozzolo2017realistic,rymantubb2018survey,hoppner2021idcs}.
\citet{jesus2022turning} introduce the BAF suite specifically to address the
lack of controlled shift benchmarks in fraud detection, offering five variants
with calibrated degrees of covariate shift, label shift, and bias injection
relative to a shared Base distribution.
Their evaluation focuses on in-domain performance and fairness metrics, leaving
cross-model cross-domain robustness as an open problem.
\citet{hoppner2021idcs} study temporal distribution shift in credit card fraud
and find that models retrained on recent data degrade less than static models;
they do not, however, compare different model families' inherent robustness
to shift when retraining is not feasible --- the typical regime when a model
is deployed at a partner institution or when retraining latency exceeds the
drift rate.

The theoretical literature on ensemble methods provides a clear prior on which
architectures should be more robust to shift.
\citet{breiman2001random} derives bias--variance decompositions showing that
bagging-based ensembles --- of which Random Forest is the canonical instance ---
reduce variance through bootstrap sampling and random feature subsampling
without changing bias.
Under distribution shift, the prediction error of a model trained on the
source distribution decomposes into a base error term and a shift-induced
error term proportional to how strongly the model has overfitted to the
specific feature co-occurrence patterns of the source distribution.
Bagging's random feature subsampling at each split forces individual trees
to make predictions without access to the full feature set, which in
expectation reduces the model's dependence on any single feature combination
and therefore reduces the shift-induced error term.
Gradient-boosted decision trees, by contrast, grow each tree on the full
gradient signal from the source distribution and can exploit any feature
combination that improves training loss, including combinations that are
specific to the source distribution and break under shift.
The result is a theoretically predicted ordering: bagging-based ensembles
should be more cross-domain robust than gradient-boosted ensembles when no
retraining is possible.
This prediction is well-established in synthetic-shift literature but has not,
to our knowledge, been confirmed on a controlled fraud benchmark with multiple
distributional variants.
The cross-domain results in Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain} provide exactly
that confirmation.

The question of how attention-based tabular models behave under distribution
shift is, in turn, almost entirely unstudied.
Tabular transformers are trained on the joint distribution of feature tokens
via self-attention, and there is no equivalent ``forced ignorance'' mechanism
analogous to random subsampling.
A first-principles expectation is that attention-based models should be more
sensitive to shift than RF and at least as sensitive as gradient-boosted trees,
but no published study tests this directly on fraud data.
Our cross-domain experiment fills this gap.

\subsection{Attribution Interpretability and Convergent Validity}
\label{sec:rw_interp}

SHAP~\citep{lundberg2017unified} provides model-agnostic Shapley value
estimates with efficient model-specific implementations: TreeExplainer for
tree-based models~\citep{lundberg2020local}, LinearExplainer for linear models,
and GradientExplainer for neural networks.
SHAP has been widely applied in fraud detection to identify important features
at the global and local level~\citep{hilal2022financial,nesvijevskaia2021accuracy},
but two questions of central deployment relevance have not been studied in a
controlled manner.
First, \emph{cross-model consistency}: whether different model architectures
trained on the same data agree on which features carry the fraud signal.
Disagreement would indicate that at least one method is confounded by
architectural biases or by the explainer's own independence assumptions
(TreeExplainer's path-dependent estimation differs from GradientExplainer's
sampling-based estimation in well-known ways).
Second, \emph{cross-domain stability}: whether the feature ranking produced
on the training distribution survives realistic shifts in the data-generating
process.
A model whose explanations change under shift is operationally fragile, even
if its predictive performance does not degrade.
Both questions are addressed in Section~\ref{sec:shap}.

Attention weights as a source of explanations have a contested history.
\citet{vaswani2017attention} introduce self-attention as a representational
mechanism without claiming it provides interpretability, and subsequent work in
NLP has shown that attention weights do not always correlate with gradient-based
or perturbation-based importance measures~\citep{molnar2022interpretable}.
On tabular transformers, the picture is different in one important respect: each
token corresponds bijectively to a single feature (or to the \texttt{[CLS]}
classification token), so attention weights have a natural per-feature
interpretation that is unavailable in token-based NLP models where one token
may participate in many distinct linguistic roles.
Tabular attention can therefore be examined diagnostically, distinguishing
qualitatively different failure modes that summary accuracy metrics would not
reveal: Tunnel Vision (concentration on few tokens), Self-Obsession
(\texttt{[CLS]} attends to itself), Categorical Trap (disproportionate
categorical attention), and Foggy Vision (near-uniform attention across
tokens, implying that the model finds no token systematically more informative
than another).

Despite these methodological foundations, no published study has simultaneously
applied SHAP attribution and attention diagnostics to the same fraud detection
model on the same data and tested whether the two architecturally distinct
methods converge on the same feature signals.
This kind of cross-method validation is known as \emph{convergent validity},
a research-design principle imported from psychometric measurement theory
where it has long been used to establish that two independent measurement
instruments are measuring the same underlying construct.
When two independent attribution methods --- with different formal underpinnings,
different implementation paths, and different failure modes --- identify the
same feature ranking on the same data, the conclusion that those features
carry genuine signal is substantially more credible than if it rested on either
method alone.
Conversely, divergent attributions would identify which features are
architecture-specific rather than data-intrinsic, an actionable diagnostic that
neither method can provide on its own.
Section~\ref{sec:attention} reports the convergent-validity result for this
paper's models and discusses its implications for the BAF performance ceiling.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

The shared experimental protocol --- datasets, model hyperparameter search,
leakage-free evaluation, and class imbalance strategies --- is described in full
in the companion paper~\citep{caixeiro2025threshold}.
We summarise here only the components that are specific to the analyses in this paper.

\subsection{Datasets}

\paragraph{Bank Account Fraud (BAF) Suite.}
The BAF suite~\citep{jesus2022turning} provides six synthetic bank account fraud
datasets with a shared core feature space of $30$--$32$ columns.
BAF~Base is the primary training and in-domain evaluation dataset
(approximately $1\,000\,000$ samples, $\approx 1.1\%$ fraud rate, $32$ features).
Variants~I--V share the core $30$ features but differ in their distributional
properties along calibrated dimensions of group-size disparity, prevalence
disparity, separability disparity, and temporal/training bias, as defined by
the original authors~\citep{jesus2022turning}.
Two of the variants (III and V) include the two auxiliary columns
\texttt{x1} and \texttt{x2}; these columns are excluded from the cross-domain
experiments of Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain} so that all models, trained on
Base, receive a uniform $30$-feature input across every variant.
We refer the reader to~\citet{jesus2022turning} for the exact specification of
each variant; the present paper treats the variants as five black-box
distributional shifts of varying severity, since our claims concern only how
the model families' performance responds to those shifts in aggregate.
The ULB 2013 dataset~\citep{dalpozzolo2015calibrating} is used only in the
imbalance sensitivity analysis (Section~\ref{sec:imbalance_sensitivity}),
providing a second dataset with a different prevalence ($0.17\%$) and a
PCA-anonymised feature space.

\subsection{Models}

Six model families are compared: Logistic Regression (LR), Random Forest (RF),
LightGBM (LGBM), CatBoost, FT-Transformer, and One-Class SVM (OCSVM).
Full hyperparameter search spaces and Optuna TPE optimisation details
are given in the companion paper~\citep{caixeiro2025threshold}.
For the imbalance sensitivity and cross-domain analyses, attention focuses on
the FT-Transformer~\citep{gorishniy2021revisiting} as the primary deep learning
representative, CatBoost as the strongest tree-based competitor, RF as the
cross-domain candidate, and LGBM as the interpretability reference.
OCSVM is included in the cross-domain tables for completeness.

\subsection{Cross-Domain Transfer Protocol}

Each model is trained on the BAF~Base training partition (stratified holdout, $80\%$
train / $20\%$ test) and applied \emph{without retraining or re-tuning} to
the holdout test sets of Variants~I--V.
The decision threshold $\tau$ calibrated on the Base validation fold
(maximising $F_2$; $\tau \approx 0.058$ for FT-Transformer) is transferred
unchanged to all variants.
Performance is reported in PR-AUC, $F_2$, and ROC-AUC; the in-domain
Base column repeats the standard holdout result as the upper reference.

\subsection{SHAP Analysis}

SHAP explanations are computed on the BAF~Base holdout test set using the
explainer best matched to each model's architecture:
LinearExplainer for Logistic Regression, TreeExplainer~\citep{lundberg2020local}
for LGBM and CatBoost, and GradientExplainer for FT-Transformer.
For categorical features one-hot encoded during preprocessing, SHAP values
are aggregated back to the original feature level (sum of absolute values across
one-hot columns) before ranking.
Two consistency analyses are reported: cross-model Jaccard similarity of the
top-$10$ feature sets across all four models on BAF~Base, and cross-variant
Jaccard similarity of LGBM's top-$10$ features across all six BAF datasets.

\subsection{Attention Diagnostic Framework}

FT-Transformer attention weights are extracted from the final self-attention
layer for all $200\,000$ BAF~Base test samples using the best-configuration model.
The model operates on $31$ tokens: $25$ numerical features, $5$ categorical
features (after embedding), and the \texttt{[CLS]} classification token.
Four diagnostic patterns are distinguished:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item \textbf{Tunnel Vision} --- concentration on a small token subset;
  \item \textbf{Self-Obsession} --- \texttt{[CLS]} attends primarily to itself;
  \item \textbf{Categorical Trap} --- categorical tokens receive disproportionately
    high attention relative to numerical ones;
  \item \textbf{Foggy Vision} --- near-uniform attention across all tokens.
\end{itemize}
The diagnostic is quantified via four statistics:
normalised entropy $H_{\text{norm}} = H / \ln N$ (where $N=31$),
\texttt{[CLS]} self-attention fraction, categorical/numerical attention ratio,
and the mean attention of the top-$3$ tokens.

On ULB~2013 the two architectures follow broadly similar profiles: Random
Undersampling (RUS) degrades both models sharply, while the remaining strategies
cluster near the baseline.
Notably, the FT-Transformer's $F_2$ standard deviation across strategies ($0.044$)
is \emph{lower} than CatBoost's ($0.060$), indicating that on this compact,
well-separated dataset the transformer is marginally more stable under resampling.

The picture reverses on BAF~Base.
The two models enter the comparison on equal footing --- both reach PR-AUC
$= 0.180$ and $F_2 \approx 0.315$ under the baseline (no intervention) --- but
their reactions to the data-level interventions diverge substantially.
SMOTE and SMOTE+Tomek reduce FT-Transformer PR-AUC by $41\%$ ($0.180 \to 0.106$),
while the same interventions reduce CatBoost only by $14\%$ ($0.180 \to 0.154$).
CatBoost's $F_2$ standard deviation ($0.006$) is four times smaller than
the FT-Transformer's ($0.024$), confirming that the asymmetry is systematic
across operational metrics, not only in PR-AUC.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Architectural Sensitivity to Synthetic Oversampling}
\label{sec:disc_smote}

The $41\%$ SMOTE-induced PR-AUC drop for FT-Transformer on BAF
(vs.\ $14\%$ for CatBoost) has a straightforward mechanistic interpretation.
SMOTE generates synthetic minority samples by linear interpolation between
existing fraud instances in the feature space~\citep{chawla2002smote}.
The FT-Transformer's attention mechanism processes each sample's feature
tokens and learns weights conditioned on the \emph{joint distribution} of
all $30$ features presented during training.
Synthetic interpolates alter this joint distribution: convex combinations
of rare fraud instances produce feature vectors that are geometrically feasible
but distributional anomalies --- combinations of feature values that do not
occur in the real data-generating process.
Attention layers trained on this distorted distribution generalise poorly
to test samples drawn from the genuine distribution, because the
per-feature attention weights are calibrated to the synthetic manifold rather
than the real one.

Tree-based models, by contrast, learn by axis-aligned splits on individual
feature values, not on continuous representations of the entire feature vector.
CatBoost's gradient boosting procedure is sensitive to the conditional distribution
of the target given each feature's splitting threshold, which is less distorted
by SMOTE interpolates than the global joint distribution seen by attention.
This explains both the asymmetry in the SMOTE effect and the fact that
Class Weights --- which modifies the loss function without altering the input
distribution --- is the safest intervention for FT-Transformer on both datasets.

On ULB, the reversal of the stability comparison (FT-T is marginally more stable
than CatBoost) is also explicable: ULB~2013's PCA-anonymised features
(V1--V28) have a well-separated, continuous structure where SMOTE interpolates
stay close to the genuine fraud manifold, so the joint-distribution distortion
is minor.
BAF's categorical-heavy feature space (five categorical features with binary
or low-cardinality values) is far more sensitive to interpolation: synthetic
samples created by averaging \texttt{device\_os} or \texttt{housing\_status}
values produce fractional encodings that are nonsensical in the original
feature space, amplifying the distributional mismatch for attention layers.

A second mechanistic layer concerns the FT-Transformer's tokenisation itself.
The architecture applies a learned linear projection to each numerical feature
to produce its token, and a learned embedding lookup for each categorical
feature.
Under SMOTE, a synthetic fraud instance with feature vector $\alpha x_i + (1-\alpha) x_j$
yields fractional one-hot values for any categorical column that differs between
$x_i$ and $x_j$, and the embedding lookup --- which expects integer indices ---
is then applied to these synthetic indices via the same linear interpolation.
The resulting ``synthetic category embedding'' lies in a region of embedding
space that corresponds to no real category, and the attention layers receive
token representations that they will never encounter at deployment.
Tree-based models do not suffer this representational discontinuity: a split on
\texttt{device\_os} either passes the value to one child or the other based on
threshold, and intermediate ``fractional'' values arising from SMOTE are simply
routed by their nearest integer category --- a graceful degradation rather than
a representational rupture.
The asymmetry is therefore not a mere accident of optimisation: it is a
predictable consequence of how transformer-style tokenisation interacts with
synthetic minority oversampling on categorical-heavy feature spaces.
The practical takeaway is that the FT-Transformer must be paired with
non-distributional imbalance interventions (Class Weights, focal loss) rather
than with data-level resampling --- a constraint that GBDTs do not face.

\subsection{Random Subsampling as Cross-Domain Regularization}
\label{sec:disc_rf}

The RF cross-domain superiority is, on its face, counter-intuitive: a model
that trails LGBM and CatBoost by $12$--$13\%$ in-domain PR-AUC improves on
four of five variants.
The explanation lies in RF's random feature subsampling ($\sqrt{p}$ features
considered at each split by default), which has a well-known regularizing
effect~\citep{breiman2001random}: each tree is forced to ignore a random subset
of features, preventing any single tree from learning an overly specialized
decision boundary that exploits feature co-occurrence patterns specific to the
training distribution.

LGBM and CatBoost grow trees on the global gradient signal, meaning their
boosted trees are allowed to use any feature combination at any split.
On BAF~Base, these models learn to exploit the joint configuration of
\texttt{device\_os}, \texttt{housing\_status}, and \texttt{phone\_home\_valid}
in a domain-specific way.
When the input distribution shifts under any of the BAF Variants ---
whether through changes in group prevalence, conditional separability, or
training bias --- those Base-specific joint configurations become less
discriminative.
Random Forest's ``forced ignorance'' of features during each tree growth
is actually advantageous here: the resulting ensemble is less dependent on
any particular feature combination and therefore more resilient when those
combinations shift.
This finding has a practical implication that runs counter to common practice:
\emph{a lower in-domain score is not a reliable proxy for cross-domain robustness};
when distribution shift is anticipated at deployment, RF should be the primary
model candidate, not the highest in-domain scorer.

The bias--variance lens makes this concrete.
For a fixed source distribution and a target distribution shifted by~$\Delta$,
the expected target-loss decomposition splits the model's error into
in-domain bias, in-domain variance, and a shift-induced cross term that
depends on how strongly the model has overfitted source-specific feature
co-occurrence~\citep{breiman2001random}.
Gradient-boosted ensembles minimise in-domain bias aggressively via
sequential boosting on the global gradient, which inflates the cross term
when~$\Delta$ is non-zero.
Bagging-based ensembles trade some in-domain bias for variance reduction
through random feature subsampling, which dampens the cross term.
On BAF, the trade is favourable: the in-domain bias cost ($0.159$ vs $0.180$
PR-AUC) is more than offset by the cross-term gain on Variants~I, II, III, IV.
This is one of two places in the analysis where a tree-based model strictly
beats the FT-Transformer (the other is CatBoost on SMOTE,
Section~\ref{sec:disc_smote}), and crucially, the result is theoretically
predicted rather than coincidental.

The cross-domain threshold transfer is also noteworthy: the threshold $\tau$
calibrated on the Base validation fold applies to all variants without recalibration.
On Variants~I and~II (where RF improves), this means that the
threshold calibrated at Base prevalence remains appropriate under variant conditions.
This is consistent with the SHAP stability finding: if the feature distributions
that drive predictions are unchanged (Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$ for LGBM), the optimal
threshold for a given feature configuration is stable across variants.

The deployment recommendation that follows is concrete and runs counter to
common ML practice.
When a fraud detection model is trained on historical data from one institution
or one time window and will be deployed where distribution shift is possible
(a new partner bank, a more recent transaction stream, a different geographic
region), the highest-scoring in-domain model is \emph{not} the right candidate.
A simpler bagging-based ensemble that scores $12$--$13\%$ lower in-domain
may produce strictly better deployed performance.
This consideration alone is sufficient to recommend Random Forest as a
production fraud detection candidate in any setting where retraining latency
exceeds the rate of distributional change --- a regime that includes most
production fraud systems we are aware of.

\subsection{Feature Attribution Stability as a Diagnostic Instrument}
\label{sec:disc_shap}

The Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$ result for LGBM across all six BAF datasets is the most
informative of the stability findings.
It does not mean that fraud is equally detectable on all variants --- it means
that \emph{the same features carry the fraud signal regardless of which variant
is evaluated}.
The performance degradation on Variants~III and~V ($0.177 \to 0.134$ and
$0.177 \to 0.122$ PR-AUC respectively for LGBM) therefore does not arise from
the model suddenly relying on different features; instead, it arises from a
change in the \emph{conditional relationship} between those stable features
and the fraud label --- which is exactly what the distributional shifts of the
BAF suite are designed to introduce.

This diagnostic capability has an immediate practical consequence: explanations
generated on the training distribution remain valid on shifted variants.
A fraud analyst relying on LGBM explanations to understand model decisions
does not need to regenerate the SHAP infrastructure when the model encounters
Variant~I or Variant~II data; the same features, in the same order, drive the same
predictions.
The FT-Transformer's lower Jaccard similarity with tree-based models ($0.54$)
is more nuanced: it does not indicate that FT-Transformer explanations are wrong,
but rather that the transformer emphasises continuous features
(\texttt{name\_email\_similarity}, \texttt{income}) more strongly than the
categorical features that dominate tree-based rankings.
This is consistent with FT-Transformer's tokenisation design, which applies a
learned linear projection to each numerical feature, providing richer gradient
signal from continuous inputs than from one-hot categorical embeddings.

For regulated and audited fraud detection deployments --- the typical setting in
banking and financial-services operations --- the SHAP stability result has
direct operational consequences.
Regulators routinely require that the features driving model predictions be
disclosed and audited, and that those features remain stable so that previously
audited explanations remain valid against newer data.
A model whose top-$10$ feature ranking changes under distribution shift would
require re-auditing every time the input distribution drifts, which is
operationally prohibitive in production fraud pipelines that face continuous
drift.
The Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$ result for LGBM on BAF demonstrates that this concern
does not materialise for tree-based fraud models: the explanation infrastructure
generated on training data remains valid on shifted data, and audited
explanations do not need to be regenerated.
The fact that this property holds for LGBM (and, by Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$ between
LGBM and CatBoost on Base, almost certainly for CatBoost as well) and is the
operational counterpart of the cross-domain robustness result of
Section~\ref{sec:disc_rf}: not only do tree-based models perform comparably or
better under shift, but their explanations also retain their validity.

\subsection{Convergent Validity and the Dataset Ceiling Interpretation}
\label{sec:disc_ceiling}

The convergent validity finding --- that LGBM SHAP attribution and FT-Transformer
attention agree on the top two features (\texttt{device\_os},
\texttt{housing\_status}) and overlap on five of ten in their respective top-$10$
rankings (Figure~\ref{fig:convergent_validity}) --- is a stronger guarantee than
either method alone could provide.
The agreement is doubly cross-method: the two attributions come from
architecturally different models (a gradient-boosted forest vs.\ a stack of
self-attention blocks) and from methodologically different attribution
estimators (Shapley value coalitional attribution vs.\ learned attention weights).
Attention weights can be confounded by architectural biases (e.g.\ higher attention
to tokens with larger embedding norms); SHAP values can be confounded by feature
correlations (e.g.\ TreeExplainer's path-dependent estimation makes different
independence assumptions than GradientExplainer's sampling-based estimation).
When two structurally different methods produce the same ranking on the same data,
the conclusion that the identified features carry genuine, robust fraud signal is
substantially more credible than if it rested on either method in isolation.

The Foggy Vision pattern ($H_{\text{norm}} = 0.976$) provides the final interpretive
piece.
FT-Transformer achieves $0.18$ PR-AUC on BAF --- identical to CatBoost --- not
because its attention mechanism is broken, degenerate, or miscalibrated.
The model integrates information from all $31$ tokens nearly uniformly precisely
because the BAF feature space does not reward selective attention: there is no
small subset of tokens that, if attended to, would yield substantially better
predictions.
The performance ceiling at $0.18$ PR-AUC is therefore a property of the feature
space and the label generating process, not of any model's inadequacy.
This interpretation is consistent with: (i) the perfect SHAP cross-variant stability
(no new features emerge as discriminative on shifted variants), (ii) the
statistical indistinguishability of CatBoost and FT-Transformer bootstrap CIs
reported in~\citet{caixeiro2025threshold}, and (iii) the negligible categorical
attention preference ($1.28\times$) which, while mechanistically expected from
FT-Transformer's tokenisation, does not translate into any measurable performance
advantage.

The practical implication is sobering but important: on BAF data with these
$30$ features, adding a more powerful model, applying a novel resampling strategy,
or extending the attention architecture is unlikely to push PR-AUC substantially
above $0.18$.
Progress on this benchmark will require either additional informative features
or a different modelling of the fraud-generating process.

The Foggy Vision result also speaks to a broader question in the tabular DL
literature: \emph{when does attention help} on tabular data?
The answer, suggested by our analysis, is that attention is a useful
architectural primitive when the feature space contains \emph{locally
discriminative structure} --- subsets of features whose informativeness depends
on the value of other features in the sample --- and is wasted in feature spaces
where each feature contributes roughly independently to the target.
In the language of~\citet{grinsztajn2022tree}, attention is helpful when
informative feature interactions exist that an axis-aligned tree split cannot
capture without combinatorial depth.
The BAF feature space does not appear to contain such structure: the
convergence of axis-aligned LGBM and self-attention FT-Transformer on the same
top features, the LGBM Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$ stability across variants, and the
identical $0.18$ PR-AUC ceiling for both models, all jointly suggest that the
fraud signal in BAF is additively contributed by a small number of high-leverage
features.
When that structural condition fails --- as it might on richer fraud feature
spaces incorporating sequence history, network features, or text-derived
embeddings --- attention may still earn its compute budget.
Foggy Vision should therefore be read not as a verdict on tabular transformers
in general, but as a \emph{useful negative result} that diagnoses when a
particular tabular feature space rewards selective attention and when it does
not.
Many published tabular DL papers report ceilings without diagnosing whether
the ceiling is the model or the data; the Foggy Vision diagnostic provides
exactly that distinction.

\subsection{Why Simple Trees Suffice: The Combined Argument}
\label{sec:disc_suffice}

The four mechanistic results of Sections~\ref{sec:disc_smote}--\ref{sec:disc_ceiling}
do not stand alone.
Taken together, they form a five-pillar argument that simple tree-based models
suffice for BAF-scale financial fraud detection.

\textbf{Pillar~1 --- No resampling penalty (\S\ref{sec:disc_smote}).}
SMOTE collapses FT-Transformer PR-AUC by $41\%$ on BAF; the same intervention
costs CatBoost only $14\%$.
The asymmetry has a principled architectural explanation in the interaction
between tokenisation and synthetic interpolates and is therefore not a
configuration-specific artefact.
A practitioner using tree-based models can apply the canonical fraud-imbalance
toolkit (SMOTE, ROS, SMOTE+Tomek) without paying an architecture-specific
penalty.

\textbf{Pillar~2 --- Cross-domain parity or advantage (\S\ref{sec:disc_rf}).}
Random Forest improves on four of five BAF cross-domain variants despite
trailing LGBM and CatBoost in-domain.
The result is theoretically predicted by the bias--variance decomposition of
bagging-based ensembles and confirmed empirically on a benchmark with
controlled distribution shift.
A practitioner who anticipates shift gains by choosing a simpler tree-based
ensemble, not a more sophisticated attention-based one.

\textbf{Pillar~3 --- Stable, deployable explanations (\S\ref{sec:disc_shap}).}
LGBM's top-$10$ SHAP features are identical (Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$) across all
six BAF datasets.
Audited explanations on training data remain valid under shift; the
explanation infrastructure does not need to be regenerated.
In regulated banking deployments, this property is itself sufficient to
recommend a tree-based model over an attention-based one whose explanation
stability under shift has not been demonstrated.

\textbf{Pillar~4 --- No headroom above the data-imposed ceiling
(\S\ref{sec:disc_ceiling}).}
The FT-Transformer's near-uniform attention (entropy $0.976$) and the
convergent agreement between attention top tokens and SHAP top features
together show that the $0.18$ PR-AUC ceiling on BAF is a signal-space
ceiling.
A more powerful architecture could exceed this ceiling only by extracting
signal that neither tree-based attribution nor self-attention identifies
as present --- a possibility this paper cannot rule out, but for which there
is no current evidence.

\textbf{Pillar~5 --- Compute is tens of times cheaper.}
The cost tables of the companion study~\citep{caixeiro2025threshold} document
that LGBM trains in $\approx 16$~seconds on BAF and tunes in $\approx 21$~minutes,
while the FT-Transformer requires $\approx 347$~seconds of training and
$\approx 13$~hours of GPU-bound tuning for an indistinguishable PR-AUC.
On equal predictive quality, the simpler model is roughly $\sim 22\times$
cheaper in training time and $\sim 37\times$ cheaper in tuning time, with no
GPU requirement.

The five pillars are not redundant.
They cover distinct dimensions: an in-domain robustness dimension (Pillar~1),
a cross-domain robustness dimension (Pillar~2), an explanation-stability
dimension (Pillar~3), an architectural-headroom dimension (Pillar~4), and a
compute dimension (Pillar~5).
The transformer architecture does not deliver a strict advantage on any of
the five.
Conversely, a tree-based ensemble --- specifically, CatBoost or LGBM for in-domain
operation, Random Forest for cross-domain operation --- meets or exceeds the
transformer on every dimension considered.
On BAF-scale financial fraud detection, this is what we mean by ``sufficient'':
no operational metric we can identify is better served by adopting an
attention-based architecture.
The conclusion is qualified --- it applies to BAF-style feature spaces and to
the FT-Transformer architecture as tested --- but it is precise enough to act
on.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

This paper showed that, on BAF-scale financial fraud detection, simple
tree-based models are sufficient.
The conclusion rests on five converging lines of evidence: (i)~SMOTE collapses
FT-Transformer PR-AUC by $41\%$ on BAF while CatBoost loses only $14\%$ ---
tree-based models survive a canonical imbalance intervention that breaks the
attention-based architecture; (ii)~Random Forest improves on four of five BAF
cross-domain variants despite a lower in-domain score, confirming a
theoretically predicted advantage of bagging's feature subsampling under shift;
(iii)~LGBM's SHAP top-$10$ feature ranking is identical across all six BAF
datasets (Jaccard\,$=\,1.00$), so audited tree-based explanations remain valid
without recomputation under distribution shift; (iv)~the FT-Transformer's
near-uniform attention (entropy $0.976$, Foggy Vision) and its convergent
agreement with SHAP on the same top features identify the $0.18$ PR-AUC ceiling
as a property of the feature space, ruling out architectural-improvement
gains; and (v)~LGBM requires roughly $20\times$ less training time and
$37\times$ less hyperparameter-tuning time than the FT-Transformer for
indistinguishable predictive quality, with no GPU dependency.

The methodological contribution of the paper is the application of
convergent validity --- a research design principle from psychometric
measurement theory --- to the comparison of SHAP attribution and self-attention
weights as fraud-feature signals on the same data.
Because the two attributions come from architecturally different models
(LGBM and FT-Transformer) and from methodologically different estimators
(Shapley values and learned attention weights), their convergence is
unusually robust evidence that the identified features carry genuine fraud
signal rather than method-specific artefacts.
The convergent finding (five overlapping features in the top-$10$, two
identical at the top --- \texttt{device\_os} and \texttt{housing\_status}) ties
the in-domain SHAP results to the attention diagnostics and bounds the
architectural-improvement headroom above the observed performance ceiling.
We are not aware of prior fraud literature that combines these two
attribution methods on the same data under a convergent-validity framing.

For practitioners, the principal recommendations are direct: prefer CatBoost or
LGBM for in-domain deployment on BAF-scale data; choose Random Forest when
cross-domain robustness is the primary concern; avoid SMOTE entirely when
using FT-Transformer (use Class Weights or focal loss instead); rely on tree-based
SHAP explanations across distributional variants without recomputation; and do
not adopt an attention-based architecture in expectation of a feature-space
advantage that the convergent validity result rules out on this benchmark.

Three limitations bound these conclusions.
First, both datasets are either PCA-anonymised (ULB) or synthetically generated
(BAF), so findings may not transfer to production fraud streams with richer
feature semantics, adversarial label noise, or temporal feedback loops.
Second, the attention diagnostic is computed on a single FT-Transformer
configuration with the hyperparameters selected by Optuna; the Foggy Vision
pattern may not generalise to architectures with more attention heads, deeper
stacks, or different tokenisation strategies (SAINT, TabTransformer).
Third, the cross-domain evaluation is conducted within the BAF suite (Base to
Variants~I--V), which is a controlled but ultimately narrower robustness
evaluation than cross-dataset transfer with matched feature spaces would offer.
A natural extension is to apply the same diagnostic framework to additional
tabular transformer architectures and to real-world fraud datasets where the
feature-space ceiling can be tested against independent external evidence,
and to deploy the convergent-validity methodology on other tabular benchmarks
where attention-based models are competitive with tree-based ones, in order to
test whether the Foggy Vision verdict generalises beyond fraud data.
