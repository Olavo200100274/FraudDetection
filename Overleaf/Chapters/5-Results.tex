This chapter presents the empirical findings of the thesis. Each section addresses a specific experimental question and is built around the artefacts (tables, figures, metric files) produced by the corresponding pipeline run. The order of sections mirrors the way the experiments build on one another: the baseline comparison (Section~\ref{sec:baseline_results}) establishes a reference point for every supervised model and OCSVM; the threshold sensitivity study (Section~\ref{sec:threshold_sensitivity}) and the full factorial analysis (Section~\ref{sec:factorial_results}) then probe, in turn, the two design choices with the largest effect on operational behaviour; transformer-specific robustness (Section~\ref{sec:transformer_imbalance}) and cross-domain transfer (Section~\ref{sec:cross_domain_results}) test the limits of the best classifiers under perturbations of the input distribution; SHAP interpretability (Section~\ref{sec:shap_results}) and attention diagnostics (Section~\ref{sec:attention_diagnostics}) explain the mechanisms behind the numbers; and the consolidated summary (Section~\ref{sec:consolidated}) packages the evidence into a practitioner-facing decision framework supported by bootstrap and computational-cost statistics. \section{Baseline Model Comparison (No Imbalance Strategy)}
\label{sec:baseline_results}

The baseline establishes the performance ceiling each model can reach when only hyperparameter tuning is applied --- without resampling, cost-sensitive weighting, or any other class-imbalance intervention. Every model is tuned via Optuna TPE (50 trials, 5-fold stratified cross-validation, PR-AUC objective) and evaluated on the holdout test set using the $F_2$-maximising threshold~($\tau$) derived from inner validation (median across folds). The trained models and probability scores produced here are reused without retraining in Section~\ref{sec:threshold_sensitivity}, so that any subsequent variation in operational metrics is attributable to the threshold rule alone.

Whether the rankings survive perturbations of either choice is the question addressed next.

\section{Threshold Sensitivity Analysis}
\label{sec:threshold_sensitivity}

The four threshold strategies introduced in Section~\ref{sec:threshold_study} of the Methodology --- fixed $\tau = 0.5$, max-$F_1$, max-$F_2$, and the lowest $\tau$ ensuring $\text{Precision} \geq 0.5$ --- are applied here to the probability scores produced by the baseline models. No retraining takes place, so any change in the operational metrics is attributable exclusively to the threshold rule. All four thresholds are selected on the validation folds (median across folds) and only then applied to the holdout test set. The analysis is structured around two questions: (i) does the choice of threshold affect $F_2$ in a way that exceeds the model-to-model gap; and (ii) does it preserve or reorder the in-domain model ranking?

On ULB, threshold choice has a moderate but meaningful effect. The tree-based models (RF, LGBM, CatBoost) are already close to optimal under the na\"ive $\tau = 0.5$ rule because their predicted probabilities are well calibrated on this dataset: most genuine frauds exceed $0.5$ outright, leaving only marginal $F_2$ headroom for explicit optimisation. Logistic Regression, in contrast, gains substantially when the threshold is calibrated --- $F_2$ rises from $0.664$ under the fixed rule to $0.799$ under max-$F_2$, a $20\%$ relative improvement that closes most of the gap to the non-linear models. The FT-Transformer behaves much like the tree ensembles, with its fixed and max-$F_1$ values numerically coinciding ($F_2 \approx 0.82$), consistent with well-separated scores around $0.5$. The Precision\,$\geq$\,0.5 strategy yields the most conservative predictions (lowest alert rates) but at the cost of a clear recall and $F_2$ penalty for every supervised model except OCSVM, where it is dominated by every alternative. Crucially, the model ranking is preserved across all four strategies on ULB: CatBoost\,$>$\,LGBM\,$\approx$\,FT-Trans.\,$\approx$\,RF\,$>$\,LR\,$\gg$\,OCSVM. Threshold choice on ULB therefore tunes the operating point without changing which model would be selected.

The fixed $\tau = 0.5$ rule is catastrophic on BAF: every supervised model collapses to $F_2 < 0.05$, and Random Forest effectively stops predicting fraud at all ($F_2 = 0.001$, Recall $= 0.0005$). The mechanism is straightforward: with $\approx 1.1\%$ fraud prevalence the score distribution concentrates well below $0.5$, so a na\"ive threshold discards nearly every positive prediction. The max-$F_2$ rule recovers performance dramatically --- CatBoost from $0.037$ to $0.314$, LGBM from $0.042$ to $0.316$, and FT-Transformer from $0.035$ to $0.317$, an almost nine-fold improvement. The max-$F_1$ rule sits between the two extremes, accepting higher precision at the cost of recall, and the Precision\,$\geq$\,0.5 rule is so conservative that OCSVM cannot satisfy it at any threshold ($\tau = \infty$ in the implementation), confirming that the unsupervised baseline is not merely weak but unable to reach a useful precision floor on this dataset. The Alert Rate columns make the workload consequence visible: moving from fixed to max-$F_2$ raises the alert rate from near zero to $3$--$4\%$ for every supervised model, which is the operational cost of recovering most of the fraud the model is capable of detecting.

A second, subtler observation concerns rankings. Where on ULB the four threshold rules preserve the model ordering, on BAF Base they reshuffle the top three: under fixed $\tau = 0.5$, LGBM leads ($F_2 = 0.042$); under max-$F_2$, FT-Transformer takes the top ($0.317$); under max-$F_1$, CatBoost dominates ($0.274$). Any conclusion about ``which model is best'' on BAF therefore depends on which threshold rule the comparison uses --- a methodological consideration that the leakage-free protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} makes explicit. The headline finding of this section is unambiguous: on a dataset with $\approx 1\%$ fraud prevalence, the threshold rule can change $F_2$ by an order of magnitude, an effect that comfortably exceeds the gap between any two supervised models in Section~\ref{sec:baseline_results}.

\section{FT-Transformer Attention Diagnostics}
\label{sec:attention_diagnostics}

The attention mechanism is the defining component of the FT-Transformer architecture, and analysing the learned weights provides a mechanistic account of how the model processes tabular features --- in particular, whether any pathological attention pattern explains the absolute performance ceiling observed on BAF. The diagnostic framework distinguishes four candidate patterns:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Tunnel Vision:} attention concentrates on a small number of features, ignoring the rest.
  \item \textbf{Self-Obsession:} the \texttt{[CLS]} classification token attends primarily to itself, blocking information flow from the feature tokens.
  \item \textbf{Categorical Trap:} categorical feature tokens receive disproportionately high attention relative to numerical ones.
  \item \textbf{Foggy Vision:} attention is near-uniformly distributed across all tokens, providing no selective emphasis.
\end{itemize}

Diagnostics are computed on $200\,000$ test samples from BAF Base using the best-performing FT-Transformer configuration ($\tau = 0.058$). The model operates on $31$ tokens: $25$ numerical features, $5$ categorical features, and the \texttt{[CLS]} classification token.

\subsection{Aggregate Attention Distribution}

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/baf/attention_aggregate.png}
\captionof{figure}[Mean per-token attention on BAF Base (FT-Transformer)]{Mean attention weight per feature token aggregated over all $200\,000$ BAF Base test samples. Categorical features are shown in orange; numerical features in blue. Error bars represent standard deviation across samples. A perfectly uniform distribution would assign $1/31 \approx 3.2\%$ to each token.}
\label{fig:attention_aggregate}
\end{minipage}

Four statistics summarise the aggregate distribution shown in Figure~\ref{fig:attention_aggregate}. The normalised entropy of the attention distribution is $H_{\text{norm}} = 0.976$, meaning the observed entropy ($3.352$ nats) is $97.6\%$ of the theoretical maximum for $31$ tokens ($\ln 31 = 3.434$ nats). The \texttt{[CLS]} self-attention is $3.1\%$, indistinguishable from the uniform baseline of $1/31 \approx 3.2\%$ --- ruling out Self-Obsession. The mean attention paid to categorical vs.\ numerical tokens is $4.0\%$ vs.\ $3.1\%$ (ratio $1.28\times$), a small categorical elevation that is several orders of magnitude below what would constitute a Categorical Trap. The top three tokens by attention --- \texttt{device\_os} ($6.2\%$), \texttt{housing\_status} ($5.0\%$), \texttt{employment\_status} ($3.8\%$) --- coincide with the top SHAP features of the tree-based models reported in Section~\ref{sec:shap_results}, providing convergent evidence that these are the genuinely informative inputs in the BAF feature space. The diagnostic verdict is \textbf{Foggy Vision}: the model exhibits none of Tunnel Vision, Self-Obsession, or Categorical Trap; attention is broadly and near-uniformly spread across all $31$ tokens.

\subsection{Individual Case Attention Heatmaps}

\noindent\begin{minipage}{\linewidth}\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.80\textwidth]{figures/baf/attention_heatmap_fp.png}
\captionof{figure}[Attention matrix for a borderline False Positive case]{Full $31{\times}31$ attention matrix for a representative False Positive case (score\,$\approx\tau = 0.0584$). Each cell $(i,j)$ shows the attention weight from query token $i$ to key token $j$. \end{minipage}

The full attention matrix for a borderline False Positive (Figure~\ref{fig:attention_heatmap_fp}) is dominated by near-uniform low values, with only marginal concentration on \texttt{device\_os} and \texttt{housing\_status}. False Negative cases (scores just below threshold; see Appendix~\ref{appendixB}) exhibit the same qualitative pattern. In both groups the classification is the product of a broad, distributed aggregation of all feature representations rather than a focused appeal to one or two tokens.

The Foggy Vision diagnosis carries a specific interpretive implication. The FT-Transformer is not performing at $0.18$ PR-AUC because its attention mechanism is broken or degenerate --- no pathological pattern is present, and the model integrates information across all features. The far more parsimonious reading is that the BAF feature space does not contain enough discriminative signal to reward selective attention: there is no subset of features that, if attended to, would yield better predictions. This is consistent with the dataset-ceiling interpretation suggested by the convergence of CatBoost and FT-Transformer at exactly $0.180$ PR-AUC (Section~\ref{sec:baseline_results}) and by the perfect cross-variant SHAP stability (Section~\ref{sec:shap_results}). The small residual categorical preference ($1.28\times$) is consistent with FT-Transformer's tokenisation design --- categorical embeddings carry slightly richer discrete representations than linear numeric projections --- but does not translate into a performance advantage over tree-based models that handle the same categorical features natively via splitting.

\section{Synthesis and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:discussion}

The experimental chapter has shown that the most consequential design decision in a fraud detection pipeline is not the choice of model family but the choice of decision threshold. On a dataset where positives are rare, the difference between a na\"ive $\tau = 0.5$ and a max-$F_2$ rule selected on validation can change $F_2$ by an order of magnitude, dwarfing every model-to-model and strategy-to-strategy gap observed in the rest of the chapter. Among the supervised models, no choice ``dominates'' in an unqualified sense: CatBoost wins in-domain on the smaller dataset; CatBoost, LGBM, and FT-Transformer are statistically indistinguishable in-domain on the larger one; Random Forest is the only model that gracefully tolerates the BAF distribution shifts despite its lower in-domain score; LGBM offers the most attractive accuracy--cost--interpretability bundle. The factorial analysis additionally establishes that imbalance handling, despite its prominence in the fraud detection literature, contributes second-order improvements at best for well-tuned gradient-boosted trees on data of $\approx 1\%$ prevalence, and that Class Weights is the safest default when an intervention is genuinely necessary --- particularly for the FT-Transformer, which suffers a $41\%$ PR-AUC drop under SMOTE on BAF without any compensating gain. The interpretability and attention analyses close the loop: tree-based and attention-based models converge on the same top features, and the FT-Transformer's normalised attention entropy of $0.976$ is evidence that its parity with CatBoost on BAF reflects a feature-space ceiling rather than an architectural failure.

\subsection*{Threats to Validity}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Internal:} the hyperparameter tuning budget is fixed at $50$ Optuna trials per model and a single outer holdout split is used in place of repeated random sub-sampling. The FT-Transformer's search space is substantially larger than that of the classical models, which may therefore under-explore its potential within the same budget.
  \item \textbf{External:} ULB features are PCA-anonymised, limiting interpretability and the real-world applicability of ULB-specific findings. The BAF suite is synthetically generated, so fraud patterns may not fully reflect the complexity of production fraud streams, where label noise, adversarial adaptation, and operational feedback loops are present.
  \item \textbf{Construct:} PR-AUC is sensitive to the class prior, so comparisons of absolute PR-AUC values across datasets with different fraud rates should be made with caution. The ranking conclusions within each dataset, however, are unaffected by this consideration.
  \item \textbf{Statistical:} the bootstrap CIs assume that the holdout test set is representative of the underlying distribution. Under extreme imbalance, bootstrap resamples may occasionally contain very few positive examples, which widens the CIs and is a known limitation of the procedure.
  \item \textbf{Cross-dataset:} ULB and BAF have incompatible feature spaces, which prevents cross-architecture, cross-dataset transfer. The cross-domain study is restricted to the BAF internal transfer setting, which, while controlled, represents a narrower robustness evaluation than matched feature spaces across independent datasets would provide.
\end{itemize}
