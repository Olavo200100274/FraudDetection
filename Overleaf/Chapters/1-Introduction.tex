\section{Context}
\label{subsec:context}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Financial fraud in electronic payment systems is a persistent threat, with annual global losses estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars and an expanding attack surface as digital payment channels proliferate \cite{rymantubb2018survey}. Automated fraud detection systems trained on historical transaction data have therefore become an important component of financial institutions' defence infrastructure.

The machine learning problem underlying fraud detection is characterised by three structural challenges. First, class imbalance is extreme: fraud rates typically range from 0.1\% to 2\%, meaning that a classifier predicting all transactions as legitimate achieves over 98\% accuracy without detecting a single fraud \cite{hegarcia2009learning}. Second, the distribution of fraud patterns is non-stationary, as fraudsters adapt their behaviour in response to deployed detection systems. Third, the operational context imposes asymmetric error costs — a missed fraud carries direct financial and reputational losses, while excessive false positives burden human investigators and degrade customer experience.

Within this landscape, gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) models have consistently demonstrated strong performance on tabular financial data, and the recent emergence of transformer-based architectures for tabular data has opened the question of whether attention-based models can challenge that position \cite{shwartzziv2022tabular}. Characterising when the observed performance gap changes, and examining evidence that may help explain it, has direct implications both for practitioners selecting models and for the research community studying the inductive biases of tabular learners. This thesis addresses that question through a controlled, leakage-free experimental study spanning two benchmark datasets, six model families, and a factorial design over imbalance handling strategies.

\section{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Despite the volume of published work on fraud detection, a review of the literature reveals recurring methodological limitations that can undermine reported conclusions and hinder fair comparison between model families.

The most consequential problem is data leakage: applying oversampling techniques before the train/test split, or without isolating resampling to the training portion of each cross-validation fold, can inflate reported metrics sufficiently to reverse model rankings \cite{hayat2025leakage}. A second limitation is the widespread use of the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (ROC-AUC) as the primary evaluation metric. Under extreme class imbalance, a high ROC-AUC can coexist with poor precision--recall performance \cite{saito2015precision}. Third, the decision threshold applied to model scores is rarely treated as an explicit experimental variable; many studies either fix it at 0.5 or select it on the test set, with the latter introducing evaluation bias \cite{luzio2024decoupling}. Fourth, many comparative studies rely on a single dataset, limiting the generalisability of their conclusions \cite{dalpozzolo2017realistic}.

These limitations are not independent: a study that uses an inappropriate metric, fixes the threshold without justification, applies oversampling before splitting, and evaluates on a single dataset cannot provide reliable evidence about the relative merits of any model family. To the best of our knowledge, no published study provides a joint, leakage-free comparison of classical GBDT models and a tabular transformer on fraud-specific benchmarks under a shared outer evaluation framework. The available literature also provides limited evidence about how calibration, imbalance interventions, and model architecture relate to any observed performance difference. This thesis therefore combines controlled comparisons with post-hoc diagnostics, while treating the latter as descriptive evidence rather than proof of a causal mechanism.

\section{Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{subsec:objectives}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This thesis conducts a controlled, leakage-free comparative study of classical machine learning models and FT-Transformer for financial fraud detection under extreme class imbalance across two structurally distinct benchmark datasets. The work is organised around five research questions.

\begin{description}

  \item[\textbf{RQ1} — Comparative performance under a leakage-free protocol.]
  Under a shared leakage-free outer evaluation framework with documented model-specific validation paths, how do classical supervised models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost) and FT-Transformer compare on financial fraud detection, with OCSVM as an unsupervised reference? Does the observed performance gap depend on the dataset?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2} — Impact of threshold selection strategy.]
  How does the choice of decision threshold strategy — fixed at 0.5, maximising $F_1$, maximising $F_2$, or constrained by a minimum precision target — affect model rankings and operational metrics?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3} — Factorial interaction of imbalance strategies and model type.]
  What is the effect of seven class imbalance handling strategies on model performance across datasets, and how do these effects interact with model family? Do resampling strategies affect GBDT and transformer models differently?

  \item[\textbf{RQ4} — Cross-domain generalisation under distribution shift.]
  Do models trained on BAF Base generalise to the controlled group-size, prevalence, separability, and temporal conditions represented by the BAF Variants without retraining, and which model families retain the strongest performance?

  \item[\textbf{RQ5} — Feature attribution consistency and transformer attention diagnostics.]
  To what extent do the top-feature sets produced by Shapley additive explanations (SHAP) agree across the linear and tree-based models, and does the LGBM top-feature set remain stable across BAF Variants? What descriptive evidence do FT-Transformer attention patterns provide about how the architecture processes the BAF features?

\end{description}

\section{Research Approach}
\label{subsec:workflow}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To address the five research questions, this thesis uses a structured experimental pipeline across two benchmark datasets: the ULB Credit Card Fraud dataset \cite{dalpozzolo2017realistic} and the Bank Account Fraud (BAF) suite \cite{jesus2022turning}. Six model families are evaluated: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost, One-Class SVM, and FT-Transformer. The five supervised models participate in the full factorial comparison of seven class imbalance handling strategies, whereas OCSVM is evaluated separately as an unsupervised anomaly-detection baseline.

All experiments use a leakage-free protocol in which an outer stratified 80/20 holdout separates the test set from training, tuning, resampling, and threshold selection. The primary evaluation metric is the area under the precision--recall curve (PR-AUC), and threshold selection is treated as a separate experimental variable. Cross-domain generalisation is assessed by applying models trained on BAF Base to each of the five BAF Variants without retraining. Interpretability is analysed using SHAP for Logistic Regression, LightGBM, and CatBoost \cite{lundberg2017unified}; FT-Transformer attention is examined separately as a descriptive architectural diagnostic.

\section{Document Structure}
\label{subsec:structure}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:stateoftheart} — State of the Art} (p.~\pageref{sec:stateoftheart}) surveys the literature across five dimensions: fraud detection benchmarks and datasets, evaluation methodology under class imbalance, classical machine learning for fraud detection, transformer-based tabular models and the GBDT vs.\ deep learning debate, and explainability techniques. The chapter concludes with a synthesis of methodological limitations and the research gaps that motivate this work.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterTheoreticalBackground} — Theoretical Background} (p.~\pageref{sec:chapterTheoreticalBackground}) collects the toolkit on which the experimental design rests: the model families, the class-imbalance handling techniques, the evaluation metrics, the decision-threshold rules, the hyperparameter optimisation procedure, and the SHAP family of feature-attribution estimators. Each tool is defined once in general terms so that subsequent chapters can reference these definitions rather than restate them.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterMethodology} — Methodology} (p.~\pageref{sec:chapterMethodology}) describes how the toolkit from the preceding chapter was configured and applied in this specific study: the two datasets, the selection rationale for the six model families, the seven imbalance strategies and four threshold rules, the model-specific validation and preprocessing paths, the leakage-free evaluation protocol, the hyperparameter tuning strategy, the cross-domain generalisation protocol on the BAF Variants, the metric reporting choices, and the SHAP and attention analyses.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterResults} — Results and Discussion} (p.~\pageref{sec:chapterResults}) follows the research questions: the baseline comparison addresses RQ1; threshold sensitivity addresses RQ2; the factorial and focused FT-Transformer analyses address RQ3; BAF transfer addresses RQ4; and the SHAP and attention analyses address RQ5. A consolidated decision framework and threats-to-validity discussion then integrate the evidence.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} — Conclusion} (p.~\pageref{sec:conclusion}) summarises the contributions of the thesis, provides direct answers to the five research questions, discusses limitations of the work, and identifies directions for future research.
