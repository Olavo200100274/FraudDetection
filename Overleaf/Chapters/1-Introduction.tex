\section{Context}
\label{subsec:context}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Financial fraud in electronic payment systems is a persistent and escalating threat to the global economy, with annual losses consistently estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars and a steady expansion in attack surface as digital payment channels proliferate \cite{rymantubb2018survey}. Card-not-present fraud, account takeover, and synthetic identity fraud exploit the scale and speed of modern payment infrastructure in ways that manual review processes cannot address at operational velocity \cite{hilal2022financial}. Automated fraud detection systems, trained on historical transaction data, have become a critical component of financial institutions' defence infrastructure.

The machine learning problem underlying fraud detection is characterised by three structural challenges that distinguish it from standard classification benchmarks. First, class imbalance is extreme: fraud rates typically range from 0.1\% to 2\%, meaning that a naive classifier predicting all transactions as legitimate achieves accuracy above 98\% without detecting a single fraud \cite{hegarcia2009learning}. Second, the distribution of fraud patterns is non-stationary: fraudsters adapt their behaviour in response to deployed detection systems, inducing concept drift that degrades model performance over time \cite{jesus2022turning}. Third, the operational context imposes asymmetric error costs — a missed fraud imposes direct financial and reputational losses, while an excessive false-positive rate burdens human investigators and degrades customer experience \cite{nesvijevskaia2021accuracy}.

Against this background, the machine learning research community has produced a substantial body of work on fraud detection methods, spanning classical ensemble models, deep learning architectures, and hybrid approaches. The dominant empirical finding, consistent across benchmark studies, is that gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) models — in particular LightGBM \cite{ke2017lightgbm} and CatBoost \cite{prokhorenkova2018catboost} — deliver state-of-the-art performance on tabular financial data \cite{thimonier2024comparative, leevy2023binary}. At the same time, the transformer architecture \cite{vaswani2017attention}, which has driven step-change improvements in natural language processing and computer vision, has been adapted for tabular data in architectures such as TabTransformer \cite{huang2020tabtransformer}, SAINT \cite{somepalli2021saint}, and FT-Transformer \cite{gorishniy2021revisiting}. Whether these architectures can match or exceed GBDT performance on fraud-specific tabular data remains an open question with direct practical implications.

\section{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Despite the volume of published work on fraud detection, a systematic review of the literature reveals recurring methodological limitations that undermine the reliability of reported conclusions and prevent a fair comparison between model families.

The most consequential problem is \textbf{data leakage}: the application of oversampling techniques such as SMOTE \cite{chawla2002smote} prior to the train/test split, or within cross-validation folds without isolating resampling to the training portion, inflates reported performance metrics by margins sufficient to reverse model rankings \cite{hayat2025leakage}. A second limitation is the widespread use of ROC-AUC as the primary evaluation metric, which is systematically misleading under extreme class imbalance: the large number of true negatives inflates the true negative rate regardless of model behaviour on the minority class, making high ROC-AUC values compatible with near-zero fraud recall \cite{saito2015precision, imani2026why}. Third, the decision threshold applied to model output probabilities — which determines the operational trade-off between precision and recall — is rarely treated as an explicit experimental variable; most studies either fix it at 0.5 without justification or select it on the test set, introducing a further form of evaluation bias \cite{luzio2024decoupling}. Fourth, the overwhelming majority of comparative studies are conducted on a single dataset, most often the ULB Credit Card Fraud benchmark \cite{dalpozzolo2017realistic}, limiting the generalisability of conclusions drawn from a two-day transaction snapshot with PCA-anonymised features.

Beyond methodological inconsistencies, a specific research gap motivates the central experimental question of this thesis. The tabular machine learning literature \cite{shwartzziv2022tabular, borisov2022deep} provides evidence that well-tuned GBDT models match or outperform tabular deep learning methods on general benchmarks — yet the extent to which this holds under the specific conditions of financial fraud detection (extreme imbalance, regulated context, mixed numeric and categorical features) has not been established in a controlled, leakage-free study that simultaneously evaluates both model families. More importantly, the \emph{reasons} behind any observed performance gap — whether attributable to calibration differences, sensitivity to imbalance strategies, architectural mismatch between attention and permutation-invariant tabular features, or dataset ceiling effects — have not been examined mechanistically. Understanding why a simple gradient-boosted model performs comparably to or better than a sophisticated tabular transformer is scientifically more valuable than reporting the gap alone \cite{shwartzziv2022tabular}.

\section{Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{subsec:objectives}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This thesis conducts a rigorous, leakage-free comparative study of classical machine learning models and the FT-Transformer architecture for financial fraud detection under extreme class imbalance, across two structurally distinct benchmark datasets. The work is organised around five research questions, each addressing a specific gap identified in the state of the art.

\begin{description}

  \item[\textbf{RQ1} — Comparative performance under a leakage-free protocol.]
  Under an identical, leakage-free evaluation protocol applied consistently across model families and datasets, how do classical supervised models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost) and the FT-Transformer compare on financial fraud detection? Does the performance gap between GBDTs and the transformer depend on the dataset characteristics (ULB vs.\ BAF)?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2} — Impact of threshold selection strategy.]
  How does the choice of decision threshold strategy — fixed at 0.5, maximising F$_1$, maximising F$_2$, or constrained by a minimum precision target — affect model rankings and operational metrics? Can threshold selection be standardised as a first-class experimental variable separate from model quality?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3} — Factorial interaction of imbalance strategies and model type.]
  What is the effect of seven class imbalance handling strategies (no resampling, RUS, ROS, SMOTE, SMOTE+Tomek, SMOTEENN, class weighting) on model performance across datasets, and how do these effects interact with model architecture? Do resampling strategies affect GBDT and transformer models differently?

  \item[\textbf{RQ4} — Cross-domain generalisation under distribution shift.]
  Do fraud detection models trained on BAF Base generalise to the five BAF Variants — which exhibit label shift, covariate shift, or combinations thereof — without retraining? Which model families are most robust to distribution shift, and does cross-domain robustness correlate with in-domain performance?

  \item[\textbf{RQ5} — Feature attribution consistency and transformer attention diagnostics.]
  Do SHAP-based feature attributions agree across model families on the same dataset, and do LGBM attributions remain stable across BAF variants under distribution shift? What do FT-Transformer attention patterns reveal about how the architecture processes fraud data, and do these patterns provide a mechanistic explanation for observed performance?

\end{description}

\section{Research Approach}
\label{subsec:workflow}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To address the five research questions, this thesis designs and executes a structured experimental pipeline across two benchmark datasets: the ULB Credit Card Fraud dataset \cite{dalpozzolo2017realistic} and the Bank Account Fraud (BAF) suite \cite{jesus2022turning}. Six model families are evaluated — Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost, One-Class SVM, and FT-Transformer — under seven class imbalance handling strategies in a full factorial design. All experiments follow a leakage-free protocol: an outer stratified 80/20 holdout separates the test set from all training, tuning, resampling, and threshold selection operations; hyperparameter optimisation uses Optuna \cite{akiba2019optuna} with 5-fold inner cross-validation for classical models and a single holdout split for the FT-Transformer. The primary evaluation metric is PR-AUC; threshold selection is treated as a separate experimental variable with four strategies evaluated systematically. Cross-domain generalisation is assessed by applying models trained on BAF Base to each of the five BAF Variants without retraining. Interpretability is analysed via SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}, including cross-model feature ranking consistency (Jaccard similarity) and FT-Transformer attention diagnostics.

\section{Document Structure}
\label{subsec:structure}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:stateoftheart} — State of the Art} surveys the literature across five dimensions: fraud detection benchmarks and datasets, evaluation methodology under class imbalance, classical machine learning for fraud detection, transformer-based tabular models and the GBDT vs.\ deep learning debate, and explainability techniques. The chapter concludes with a synthesis of methodological limitations and the six research gaps that motivate this work.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterMethodology} — Methodology} details the experimental design, including the two datasets, six model families, seven imbalance strategies, leakage-free evaluation protocol, threshold selection study, hyperparameter tuning strategy, cross-domain generalisation protocol, evaluation metrics, and SHAP interpretability analysis.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterResults} — Results} presents the experimental findings in five parts: baseline model comparison under no resampling, threshold selection study, full factorial analysis of imbalance strategies, cross-domain generalisation on BAF Variants, and SHAP interpretability including FT-Transformer attention diagnostics. Each part is followed by a discussion of its implications.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} — Conclusion} summarises the contributions of the thesis, provides direct answers to the five research questions, discusses limitations of the work, and identifies directions for future research.
