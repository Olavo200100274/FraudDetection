\section{Context}
\label{subsec:context}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Financial fraud in electronic payment systems is a persistent and escalating threat, with annual global losses estimated in the hundreds of billions of dollars and a steady expansion in attack surface as digital payment channels proliferate \cite{rymantubb2018survey}. Automated fraud detection systems, trained on historical transaction data, have become a critical component of financial institutions' defence infrastructure.

The machine learning problem underlying fraud detection is characterised by three structural challenges. First, class imbalance is extreme: fraud rates typically range from 0.1\% to 2\%, meaning that a classifier predicting all transactions as legitimate achieves over 98\% accuracy without detecting a single fraud \cite{hegarcia2009learning}. Second, the distribution of fraud patterns is non-stationary, as fraudsters adapt their behaviour in response to deployed detection systems. Third, the operational context imposes asymmetric error costs — a missed fraud carries direct financial and reputational losses, while excessive false positives burden human investigators and degrade customer experience.

Within this landscape, gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) models have consistently demonstrated state-of-the-art performance on tabular financial data, and the recent emergence of transformer-based architectures for tabular data has opened the question of whether attention-based models can challenge that dominance \cite{shwartzziv2022tabular}. Understanding when and why one approach outperforms the other — not merely reporting the gap — has direct implications both for practitioners selecting models and for the research community seeking principled explanations. This thesis addresses that question through a controlled, leakage-free experimental study spanning two benchmark datasets, six model families, and a factorial design over imbalance handling strategies.

\section{Motivation}
\label{subsec:motivation}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Despite the volume of published work on fraud detection, a systematic review of the literature reveals recurring methodological limitations that undermine the reliability of reported conclusions and prevent fair comparison between model families.

The most consequential problem is data leakage: applying oversampling techniques prior to the train/test split, or without isolating resampling to the training portion of each cross-validation fold, inflates reported metrics by margins sufficient to reverse model rankings \cite{hayat2025leakage}. A second limitation is the widespread use of ROC-AUC as the primary evaluation metric, which is systematically misleading under extreme class imbalance — high ROC-AUC values are compatible with near-zero fraud recall \cite{saito2015precision}. Third, the decision threshold applied to model output probabilities is rarely treated as an explicit experimental variable; most studies either fix it at 0.5 or select it on the test set, both of which introduce evaluation bias \cite{luzio2024decoupling}. Fourth, the overwhelming majority of comparative studies rely on a single dataset, limiting the generalisability of their conclusions \cite{dalpozzolo2017realistic}.

These limitations are not independent: a study that uses an inappropriate metric, fixes the threshold without justification, applies oversampling before splitting, and evaluates on a single dataset cannot provide reliable evidence about the relative merits of any model family. Consequently, no published study provides a joint, leakage-free comparison of classical GBDT models and a tabular transformer on fraud-specific benchmarks under an identical protocol. More importantly, the \emph{reasons} behind any observed performance difference — whether attributable to calibration, sensitivity to imbalance handling strategies, or architectural mismatch between attention and permutation-invariant tabular features — have not been examined mechanistically. Understanding why a simple gradient-boosted model performs comparably to or better than a sophisticated tabular transformer is scientifically more valuable than reporting the gap alone.

\section{Objectives and Research Questions}
\label{subsec:objectives}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

This thesis conducts a rigorous, leakage-free comparative study of classical machine learning models and the FT-Transformer architecture for financial fraud detection under extreme class imbalance, across two structurally distinct benchmark datasets. The work is organised around five research questions.

\begin{description}

  \item[\textbf{RQ1} — Comparative performance under a leakage-free protocol.]
  Under an identical, leakage-free evaluation protocol, how do classical supervised models (Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost) and the FT-Transformer compare on financial fraud detection? Does the performance gap depend on dataset characteristics?

  \item[\textbf{RQ2} — Impact of threshold selection strategy.]
  How does the choice of decision threshold strategy — fixed at 0.5, maximising F$_1$, maximising F$_2$, or constrained by a minimum precision target — affect model rankings and operational metrics?

  \item[\textbf{RQ3} — Factorial interaction of imbalance strategies and model type.]
  What is the effect of seven class imbalance handling strategies on model performance across datasets, and how do these effects interact with model architecture? Do resampling strategies affect GBDT and transformer models differently?

  \item[\textbf{RQ4} — Cross-domain generalisation under distribution shift.]
  Do models trained on a source fraud dataset generalise to distribution-shifted variants without retraining, and which model families are most robust to covariate and label shift?

  \item[\textbf{RQ5} — Feature attribution consistency and transformer attention diagnostics.]
  Do SHAP-based feature attributions agree across model families, and do they remain stable across dataset variants? What do FT-Transformer attention patterns reveal about how the architecture processes fraud data?

\end{description}

\section{Research Approach}
\label{subsec:workflow}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

To address the five research questions, this thesis designs and executes a structured experimental pipeline across two benchmark datasets: the ULB Credit Card Fraud dataset \cite{dalpozzolo2017realistic} and the Bank Account Fraud (BAF) suite \cite{jesus2022turning}. Six model families are evaluated — Logistic Regression, Random Forest, LightGBM, CatBoost, One-Class SVM, and FT-Transformer — under seven class imbalance handling strategies in a full factorial design. All experiments follow a leakage-free protocol in which an outer stratified 80/20 holdout separates the test set from all training, tuning, resampling, and threshold selection operations. The primary evaluation metric is PR-AUC; threshold selection is treated as a separate experimental variable. Cross-domain generalisation is assessed by applying models trained on BAF Base to each of the five BAF Variants without retraining. Interpretability is analysed via SHAP \cite{lundberg2017unified}, including cross-model feature ranking consistency and FT-Transformer attention diagnostics.

\section{Document Structure}
\label{subsec:structure}
% ──────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The remainder of this thesis is organised as follows.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:stateoftheart} — State of the Art} surveys the literature across five dimensions: fraud detection benchmarks and datasets, evaluation methodology under class imbalance, classical machine learning for fraud detection, transformer-based tabular models and the GBDT vs.\ deep learning debate, and explainability techniques. The chapter concludes with a synthesis of methodological limitations and the research gaps that motivate this work.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterMethodology} — Methodology} details the experimental design: the two datasets, six model families, seven imbalance strategies, leakage-free evaluation protocol, threshold selection study, hyperparameter tuning strategy, cross-domain generalisation protocol, evaluation metrics, and SHAP interpretability analysis.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:chapterResults} — Results} presents the experimental findings in five parts: baseline model comparison, threshold selection study, full factorial analysis of imbalance strategies, cross-domain generalisation on BAF Variants, and SHAP interpretability including FT-Transformer attention diagnostics.

\textbf{Chapter~\ref{sec:conclusion} — Conclusion} summarises the contributions of the thesis, provides direct answers to the five research questions, discusses limitations of the work, and identifies directions for future research.
